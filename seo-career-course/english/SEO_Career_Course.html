Chapter 1

Introduction to SEO and the Career Path

Learning objectives. After this chapter you will be able to explain what SEO is, describe the work an SEO specialist does, and choose a realistic first career path.

1.1 What is SEO?

Search Engine Optimisation (SEO) is the practice of making a website easier for search engines to understand, and more useful for the people who search. When a website ranks higher in search results for the words people type, it receives more visitors at no cost per click. Those visitors can become customers, readers, subscribers or patients.

SEO is not a trick. Search engines want to show the most helpful pages. A good SEO specialist helps a site be found, understood and trusted, and helps the site owner remove the barriers that stop it from ranking.

1.2 Why SEO is a good career

  • Demand is steady. Almost every business with a website needs organic traffic, and many small businesses have never had their site checked.
  • Measurable results. You can show clients exact numbers: search impressions, clicks, positions and enquiries.
  • Low startup cost. Most core tools are free. You can start with a laptop and a good internet connection.
  • Flexible work. SEO can be done remotely, as a freelancer, inside a company, or in an agency.
  • Skills grow with AI. AI tools help with drafting and data work, but clients still need people who understand strategy, data and trust.

Chapter 2

How Search Engines Work

Learning objectives. Explain crawling, indexing and ranking; describe what a search results page contains; and name the main quality signals search engines use.

2.1 Three steps: crawl, index, rank

Search engines such as Google and Bing do three main jobs for every page they know about.

  1. Crawling — automated programs (Googlebot for Google, Bingbot for Bing) visit pages by following links and reading sitemaps.
  2. Indexing — the search engine processes the content of the page, understands its topic and stores it in a huge database called the index.
  3. Ranking — when someone searches, the engine picks matching pages from the index and orders them by relevance, quality and usability signals.

Most SEO problems fall into one of these three stages. If a page is not crawled, it cannot be indexed. If it is not indexed, it cannot rank. If it ranks but gets no clicks, the title or description may be weak, or the page may not match what people want.

2.2 What is on a search results page?

A modern results page (SERP) may show paid ads, a map pack for local businesses, featured snippets, images, videos, "People also ask" boxes and AI-generated summaries, followed by organic links. As an SEO specialist you need to know which of these your client can appear in.

2.3 Anatomy of a search snippet

2.4 Signals that matter (in plain language)

Search engines use hundreds of signals. Nobody outside the search companies knows all of them, and they change constantly. The main groups you should understand are:

GroupExamplesPlain meaning
RelevanceKeywords in title and text, topic matchDoes the page match what the person typed?
Quality and trustExpertise, author information, reviews, links from respected sitesIs this source reliable?
ExperienceCore Web Vitals, mobile use, secure HTTPS, no intrusive pop-upsIs the page pleasant to use?
ContextLocation, language, device, search history where allowedIs this useful for this person right now?
Content satisfactionAnswers the question fully, no need to search againDid the page solve the problem?
Be careful with promisesNo honest SEO specialist can guarantee a number-one ranking. Google's published guidance says that there is no shortcut. Be wary of anyone who promises guaranteed results or sells "secret" links.

2.5 Google's guidance: the rules to follow

  • Write for people first. Search engines follow the same goal: useful, original content.
  • Follow the Google Search Essentials (formerly called the Webmaster Guidelines). These describe what is allowed.
  • Avoid spam practices: hidden text, doorway pages, scaled low-quality content, buying links that pass ranking value.
  • Show experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trust (E-E-A-T). For health, finance and legal pages this matters even more.

Chapter 2 practice exercise

  1. Search for "best coffee shop in [your city]". Describe what the map pack shows and which websites appear in the organic results.
  2. Open the Google Search Essentials page and list five rules you could explain to a client.
  3. Open the source of one website (right-click, View Page Source). Find the title tag and the meta description.
SummarySearch engines crawl, index and rank pages. Titles, descriptions, helpful content, trust and page experience all influence results. Always follow the search engines' published rules.

Hardware: Building Your Workstation

Learning objectives. Choose a computer, internet connection, screens and backup setup that suit SEO work and your budget.

3.1 Why hardware matters for SEO

SEO work involves a lot of browser tabs, large spreadsheets, website crawls that can run for hours, and video calls with clients. A slow or unreliable computer wastes your time, and a crawl that fails halfway can waste a whole afternoon. You do not need a gaming machine, but you do need a dependable one.

4.2 Set up your accounts first

  1. Create one Google account for work (for example yourname.seo@gmail.com), separate from your personal account. Turn on two-step verification.
  2. Install Google Chrome and create a separate Chrome profile for each client. This keeps logins and history apart.
  3. Create a folder structure in Google Drive: Clients / [Client name] / Audits, Reports, Content, Invoices.
  4. Set up a password manager (for example Bitwarden, which has a free plan).
  5. Create a business email address with your own domain when you start getting clients. A professional address builds trust.

4.3 Install and check the core free tools

Google Search Console (GSC)

Search Console is Google's free tool that shows how your site performs in Google Search. It is the most important free tool in SEO.

  • Go to search.google.com/search-console and add a property. Use the Domain option when you can; it covers all subdomains and protocols.
  • Verify ownership with a DNS record (recommended), an HTML file, or a meta tag. Ask the client for access; never ask for their Google password.
  • Give the client's web developer or your own account only the access they need. Use Full or Restricted permissions carefully.
  • Submit the XML sitemap (for example https://example.com/sitemap.xml) from the Sitemaps report.

Google Analytics 4 (GA4)

GA4 measures visitors, sessions, events and conversions. Universal Analytics stopped processing data in 2023, so all new setups use GA4. Set up a GA4 property, install the tag through Google Tag Manager or the site's own code, and mark important actions such as form submissions, phone clicks and purchases as key events (called conversions in older guides).

Bing Webmaster Tools

Bing has a separate index and a share of search traffic that many businesses ignore. Import your Search Console site into Bing Webmaster Tools with one click, then check its reports.

PageSpeed Insights and Lighthouse

Enter any page address at pagespeed.web.dev to get lab and field data on speed and Core Web Vitals. Lighthouse is built into Chrome DevTools (press F12, open the Lighthouse tab) and gives the same kind of report locally.

Screaming Frog SEO Spider

Screaming Frog is a desktop crawler that visits each URL on a site and reports titles, meta descriptions, headings, status codes, redirects, images and links. The free version crawls up to 500 URLs; the paid licence removes this limit. Install it on Windows, macOS or Linux, and allow it at least 2 to 4 GB of memory for medium-sized sites (Configuration > System > Memory Allocation).

Google Keyword Planner and Google Trends

Keyword Planner is inside Google Ads. You can use it without spending money by creating a Google Ads account and skipping the campaign setup. Google Trends shows relative search interest over time and by region; it is free and needs no account.

Rich Results Test and Schema Markup Validator

Use the Rich Results Test (search.google.com/test/rich-results) to check structured data for rich results. Use the Schema Markup Validator (validator.schema.org) to check any schema.org markup.

Looker Studio

Looker Studio (formerly Data Studio) is Google's free dashboard tool. It connects directly to Search Console, GA4 and Google Sheets. You will build client reports here in Chapter 9.

4.4 Paid tools: how to choose

Start with the free tools for your first three months. When you have a paying client who needs competitor research, large keyword lists or backlink data, consider one paid all-in-one suite. Compare:

  • Data quality for your target country and language. Ask for a trial and test it on a keyword you know.
  • Crawling limits and how many projects you can add.
  • Number of users and reports, and whether you can export data.
  • Learning resources and support. A tool you cannot use is wasted money.

4.5 Software security and privacy

  • Never share a client's Google or hosting password. Use the platform's access sharing instead.
  • Use two-step verification on every account.
  • Do not install unknown browser extensions that ask for access to "all websites" unless you know the publisher.
  • Keep client files separate. Delete temporary crawl exports when a project closes, after your client agreement allows it.

  2. Install Screaming Frog and crawl your site. Note how many URLs it finds and whether any show errors.
  3. Run PageSpeed Insights on your homepage and write down the mobile score and the three Core Web Vitals values.

Chapter 5

Keyword Research

Learning objectives. Find, group and prioritise keywords for a business; understand search intent; and turn keywords into a page plan.

5.1 What is a keyword?

A keyword is a word or phrase people type into a search engine. In SEO a keyword is also the topic a page should target. Keyword research means finding which phrases people use, how often they search, how hard it is to rank, and how close they are to buying.

5.2 Search intent: the most important idea

Search intent is the reason behind a search. A page ranks only when it matches the intent. Always check the current results for your keyword before you write anything: if all the top results are product pages, a long blog article will struggle.

5.3 Keyword metrics you will see

MetricMeaningHow to use it
Search volumeAverage monthly searches in a country (an estimate)Use as a rough guide; small businesses often do well with 10 to 500 searches a month
Keyword difficulty (KD)A score from 0 to 100 of how hard it is to rank, based on the links of current top pagesCompare within one tool only; prefer lower scores for new sites
CPC (cost per click)Average price advertisers pay for a clickHigh CPC suggests commercial value
TrendWhether search interest is rising, stable or fallingCheck Google Trends before investing in a seasonal topic
IntentInformational, navigational, commercial or transactionalMatch the page type to the intent
SERP featuresAds, maps, snippets, videos or AI overviews on the results pageDecide whether a page can realistically win a place

5.4 Step-by-step keyword research process

  1. Understand the business. Ask the client: what do you sell, to whom, in which cities, and what is your best-selling service?
  2. Brainstorm seed keywords. Write 10 to 20 phrases customers would use. Include the client's own words, competitors' names (for research only) and local terms.
  3. Expand the list. Use Google Autocomplete (type the seed and read the suggestions), the "People also ask" boxes, the "related searches" at the bottom of results, and tools such as AnswerThePublic or your paid suite.
  4. Collect data. Put all keywords into one Google Sheet. Add volume, difficulty, CPC and intent from Keyword Planner or your tool. Pull Google Trends for the top 10.
  5. Check the results. Search each important keyword in an incognito window with the client's city set (or with a location setting in your tool). Note who ranks: big brands, directories, forums, or small local sites?
  6. Group keywords into clusters. Keywords that share one search intent belong to one page. For example "dental implant cost", "how much are dental implants" and "dental implant price" usually share one pricing page.
  7. Map clusters to pages. Create a keyword map: one row per URL, with primary keyword, secondary keywords, intent and priority.

5.5 Local and language considerations

  • Search volume is per country and language. Set the correct country in every tool.
  • Many people search in mixed languages or with spelling variants. Include them: for example "electrician near me", "electrician nearby", local spellings and transliterated words.
  • For local businesses, include the city and nearby areas: "[service] in [city]", "[service] near [area]".

5.6 Worked example

A small clinic in a city wants more patients for dental implants. Seed keywords: "dental implants", "tooth implant", "implant dentist". Expanded list includes "dental implant cost", "best dentist for implants near me" and "is dental implant painful". Clusters: (1) service page for "dental implants [city]"; (2) price page for "dental implant cost"; (3) guide page for "is dental implant painful" (informational, to support the service page). Result: three planned pages, one service page with local keywords, and a list of questions for the content team.

Chapter 5 practice exercise

  1. Choose a business in your neighbourhood (or a friend's business). Write 15 seed keywords.
  2. Expand them to at least 60 keywords using autocomplete and "People also ask". Put them in a Google Sheet.
  3. Add intent to every keyword and group them into at least four clusters.
  4. Create a keyword map with one row per planned page. Include the primary keyword, secondary keywords and priority (High, Medium, Low).
SummaryKeyword research means finding phrases, understanding intent, checking real results, clustering and mapping keywords to pages. Good research prevents you from writing pages that nobody will find.
Chapter 6

Technical SEO and Site Audits

Learning objectives. Run a technical audit using Search Console, Screaming Frog and PageSpeed Insights; read the results; and write a prioritised fix list that a developer can act on.

6.1 What technical SEO covers

Technical SEO makes sure search engines can reach, read and store the right pages, and that the site works well for visitors. The main areas are:

  • Crawlability — robots.txt, internal links, no crawl traps, correct server responses.
  • Indexability — noindex tags used correctly, canonical tags, sitemap quality, duplicate content control.
  • Site structure — clear categories, logical URLs, shallow depth (important pages within three clicks from the homepage).
  • Performance — Core Web Vitals and mobile speed.
  • Security and errors — HTTPS everywhere, no broken links, correct redirects, no mixed content.
  • Structured data — schema markup that describes the business, products, articles or FAQs.

6.2 Site architecture

6.3 Core Web Vitals

Core Web Vitals are Google's user experience measures. Check them in PageSpeed Insights and Search Console (Experience > Core Web Vitals). The three metrics are:

INP replaced the older First Input Delay (FID) measure in March 2024. Thresholds are assessed on the 75th percentile of real visits, so check field data (real users) as well as lab data (a test run).

6.4 Step-by-step technical audit

Step 1 — Gather access and context

Get Search Console access, GA4 access, the CMS login (through the client's developer or a shared account), and a list of the client's most important pages.

Step 2 — Review Search Console

  • Pages report (Indexing): note "Not indexed" reasons such as "Crawled – currently not indexed", "Duplicate without user-selected canonical", "Page with redirect" and "Not found (404)".
  • Sitemaps: confirm the sitemap is submitted, fetched and contains the right number of URLs.
  • Performance: review clicks, impressions, average position and CTR for the last 3 months, with queries and pages.
  • Experience: Core Web Vitals and mobile usability issues.

Step 3 — Crawl with Screaming Frog

  1. Enter the homepage URL and start the crawl. For large sites, set a limit or crawl sections.
  2. Check the Response Codes tab: list all 4xx (broken) and 5xx (server) errors.
  3. Check Redirects: look for redirect chains (more than one hop) and redirect loops.
  4. Check Page Titles, Meta Description, H1 and Canonicals tabs for missing, duplicate or too-long values.
  5. Check Directives for pages marked noindex that should be indexed.
  6. Check Images for missing alt text and very large file sizes.
  7. Export each problem list to a spreadsheet.

Step 4 — Test speed

Run PageSpeed Insights for the homepage, one service page and one blog post, on mobile and desktop. Record the scores and the top three "opportunities" suggested.

Step 5 — Check structured data

Paste key page addresses into the Rich Results Test. Note errors (which block the rich result) and warnings (which are advisory).

Step 6 — Write the audit report

Group findings into three levels:

PriorityMeaningExampleTypical deadline
CriticalBlocks crawling, indexing or the main conversion pathWhole site set to noindex; homepage returns 500This week
HighSignificantly limits rankings or user experienceMany broken internal links; slow mobile LCP on service pagesWithin 30 days
Medium / LowImprovement with smaller impactLong titles; missing alt text on some imagesWithin 90 days

Every finding in the report needs four things: the problem, the evidence (a screenshot or a URL list), the exact fix, and who should do it.

6.5 Robots.txt, sitemaps and canonicals

  • robots.txt lives at the root of the domain (example.com/robots.txt). It tells crawlers which folders to avoid. Do not block CSS or JavaScript that the page needs to render. Do not use robots.txt to hide pages from Google; use noindex for that.
  • XML sitemap lists the important URLs you want indexed. Include only canonical, indexable pages with a 200 status code.
  • Canonical tag (<link rel="canonical" href="...">) tells search engines which version of a page is the preferred one. Use it to handle duplicate versions, such as URLs with filter parameters.
  • Redirects: use 301 (permanent) when a page has moved for good. Avoid chains. Update internal links to point to the final URL.

6.6 Structured data (schema) basics

Schema markup is code that describes your content to machines. The most common types for small businesses are LocalBusiness, Organization, Service, Product, FAQPage, Article and BreadcrumbList. Use JSON-LD format, placed in the page head or body. Google reduced the number of rich result types it shows (FAQ results are now limited to certain sites), so test before you promise results. Schema helps understanding, but it is not a guaranteed ranking boost.

Do not mark up hidden contentSchema must describe content visible to users on the page. Fake reviews or ratings are against Google's policies and can lead to manual penalties.

Chapter 6 practice exercise

  1. Crawl a site you have access to (your own or a free demo site) with Screaming Frog. Export the response codes and page titles.
  2. Find at least three broken internal links and at least one redirect chain.
  3. Run PageSpeed Insights on three page types and record the mobile LCP, INP and CLS.
  4. Write a one-page audit with a priority table of at least five findings. Include the exact fix for each.
SummaryA technical audit checks crawling, indexing, structure, speed, errors and structured data. Use Search Console for what Google sees, Screaming Frog for what the site contains, and PageSpeed Insights for experience. Report findings by priority with clear fixes.
Chapter 7

On-Page SEO and Content Optimisation

Learning objectives. Optimise titles, descriptions, headings, text, images and internal links; plan and brief content; and check the quality of a page before publishing.

7.1 The on-page checklist

On-page SEO is everything you control on a single page. Work through this list for each important page.

ElementGuidelineCommon mistake
Title tagAbout 50 to 60 characters; primary keyword near the start; brand at the endSame title on many pages; keyword stuffing
Meta descriptionAbout 120 to 160 characters; clear benefit and a call to actionCopy of the first paragraph; empty description
URL slugShort, lowercase, hyphens, contains the main keywordRandom IDs or dates for evergreen pages

H2 and H3 headingsOrganise sections; include related keywords naturallyHeadings used only to style text

ImagesDescriptive file names; alt text that describes the image; compressed (WebP or optimised JPEG)Generic names like IMG_0023.jpg; huge uncompressed images

7.2 Anatomy of a well-optimised page

7.3 Writing titles and descriptions

Write titles that a person would want to click. Use this pattern: Primary topic – Benefit or qualifier | Brand. Examples:

  • "Dental Implants in Lahore: Costs, Process and Aftercare | Example Clinic"
  • "Vegan Protein Powder Reviews 2026: 7 Tested Brands | Example Health"

Write descriptions as short promises with a reason to click: "Compare prices, see how long recovery takes and book a consultation this week."

Watch the year in titlesTitles with a year are useful for "best" or "reviews" pages, but you must update the year and the content every year. Do not put a year on evergreen service pages.

7.4 Content briefs and planning

A content brief tells a writer what to produce. A good brief includes:

  1. Target keyword, secondary keywords and search intent
  2. Audience and the problem the page solves
  3. Working title and meta description
  4. Suggested outline with H2 sections and the questions each must answer
  5. Facts, sources and experts to include; claims that need verification
  6. Internal links to add and pages to link to it
  7. Word count range (a guide, not a target); example: 800 to 1,200 words for a service page with FAQs
  8. Call to action and the business details to include

7.5 Writing for people and for search

  • Answer the question in the first 100 words, then go deeper.
  • Use short sentences and plain words. Aim for a reading age that a general audience can follow.
  • Use bullet lists and tables where they help the reader compare.
  • Show real experience: photos from the client's clinic or shop, case examples, and named staff with qualifications.
  • Update old pages. Mark the last-reviewed date on guides where accuracy matters.
  • Use AI tools only as assistants. Check every fact, price, medical or legal claim, and make sure the text sounds like the client.

7.6 Internal linking

Internal links help visitors move around and help search engines find and understand pages. Build a link plan: each service page links to its related guides and FAQs; each guide links back to its service page with a natural anchor such as "dental implant options"; the main menu and footer link to the most important sections.

Chapter 8

Off-Page SEO, Link Building and Local SEO

Learning objectives. Explain how links and mentions affect authority; earn links ethically; set up and optimise a Google Business Profile; and manage local citations.

8.1 Links as votes of trust

A link from another website to yours is a signal that the other site trusts you. Search engines weigh links by the quality and relevance of the linking site, not just the number of links. Ten links from unrelated spam sites are worth less than one link from a respected local newspaper or industry association.

8.2 Ethical link building

MethodHow it worksRisk level
Create useful resourcesPublish a guide, calculator or data study that others naturally citeLow
Digital PROffer journalists expert comments, original data or a story angleLow
Partnerships and sponsorshipsSupport a local event, school or charity; ask for a link on their siteLow
Directories and associationsListings in trusted industry directories, chambers of commerce, professional bodiesLow to medium (choose carefully)
Guest articlesWrite an expert article for a relevant site with a clear editorial policyMedium (avoid mass-produced guest posts)
Broken link buildingFind broken links on relevant sites and suggest your page as a replacementLow
Buying links, link farms, paid link networksPay for links that pass ranking valueHigh: against Google's policies; can cause penalties
Client protectionDo not build links that Google's guidelines prohibit. If a client asks you to buy links, explain the risk in writing and decline. You protect the client's long-term results.

8.3 Outreach email basics

  • Find the right person (editor, webmaster, reporter) and personalise the first line with a specific detail.
  • Offer something of value: a fact, a resource, a correction, an interview.
  • Keep it short: four to six lines. Include who you are, the reason, and a clear question.
  • Follow up once or twice after a week. Stop if the person says no.
  • Follow spam laws in the countries you email (such as CAN-SPAM in the USA and GDPR/PECR in the EU and UK): identify yourself, provide a way to unsubscribe for marketing email, and respect opt-outs.

8.4 Local SEO and the Google Business Profile

Local SEO helps businesses that serve customers in a location. The most important tool is the Google Business Profile (GBP), which controls the business panel and map results. Use the official setup at business.google.com. The main factors Google describes are relevance (how well the profile matches the search), distance (how far the business is from the searcher) and prominence (how well-known the business is online, including reviews and links).

Google Business Profile checklist

  • Use the real business name, address and phone number. Do not add keywords to the name (this breaks the rules).
  • Choose the most accurate primary category and add relevant secondary categories.
  • Add services, opening hours (including holidays), website link and appointment link.
  • Upload real photos of the place, team and products. Add new photos monthly.
  • Write a clear description that explains what the business does and where it serves.
  • Ask happy customers for reviews. Reply to every review politely, positive or negative.
  • Post updates or offers when they are relevant. Check the Insights report monthly.
  • Keep NAP (name, address, phone) identical across the website, directories and social profiles.

8.5 Citations and directories

A citation is any online mention of a business's name, address and phone number. Core citations include Google, Bing Places, Apple Maps, Facebook, and national or local directories. Use a citation checker (for example BrightLocal or Whitespark, both paid) or search manually. Fix inconsistent details first, because mismatched data confuses search engines and customers.

8.6 Measuring link and local progress

  • Use Ahrefs Webmaster Tools (free for verified sites) or the Links report in Search Console to track new and lost links.
  • Track Google Business Profile performance: searches, views, calls, direction requests and website clicks.
  • Track review count, average rating and response rate monthly.

Chapter 8 practice exercise

  1. Find a local business (a friend's shop is ideal). Search for its main service and record its position in the map pack and in organic results.
  2. Use the checklist above to audit its Google Business Profile. List the gaps.
  3. Find five relevant local directories or associations where the business could be listed. Write the NAP details you would use.
  4. Write a short outreach email to a local newspaper or association offering a useful comment or resource. Keep it to six lines.
SummaryLinks are earned through useful content and relationships, never bought. Local SEO depends on a complete, accurate Google Business Profile, consistent citations and genuine reviews.

9.2 Read the Search Console performance report

  1. Set the date range to the last 3 months and compare with the previous period.
  2. Sort queries by impressions. Look for queries with many impressions but low CTR: these pages need better titles.
  3. Look for queries ranking in positions 8 to 20 (page two). Small improvements can move them to page one.
  4. Filter by page to see which URLs gain or lose clicks.
  5. Use the "Compare" option to find pages with the biggest drop; check for technical issues, content changes or seasonal demand.

9.3 Build a monthly report in Looker Studio

9.4 A report structure that clients read

  1. Summary (one half page): three wins, two concerns, and the plan for next month.
  2. Headline numbers: clicks, impressions, CTR, enquiries, Google Business Profile actions, each with the change from last month.
  3. What changed: the work completed this month, linked to the results where possible.
  4. Top pages and keywords: the pages gaining the most, and the opportunities in positions 8 to 20.
  5. Technical health: errors fixed, errors open, Core Web Vitals status.
  6. Next month's plan: three to five actions with owner and date.
Be honest with dataSearch Console data is delayed by about two days and some queries are hidden for privacy. Changes take weeks or months to show. Explain trends rather than blaming or praising one week of data.

9.5 Common analysis mistakes

  • Judging SEO after two weeks. Most changes need at least 8 to 12 weeks.
  • Comparing average positions across very different queries.
  • Confusing impressions with visits.
  • Reporting vanity numbers (for example total pages indexed) without business outcomes.
  • Ignoring seasonality. Compare with the same month last year where possible.

10.1 Why productivity matters in SEO

SEO work is made of many small, repeated tasks: checking rankings, writing titles, answering client emails, running crawls, building reports. 10.4 Time-management methods

  • Time blocking: put deep-work blocks (audits, writing, analysis) in your calendar first. Treat them like client meetings.
  • Pomodoro: work 25 minutes, rest 5 minutes. Useful for crawl reviews and repetitive data work.
  • Batching: answer emails at two set times a day, not after every notification. Prepare all client calls for one day when possible.
  • Eat the frog: do the hardest, most valuable task first thing in the morning.
  • Weekly review (30 minutes, Friday): review what was delivered, what slipped, and the plan for next week.

10.6 Using AI tools well

AI assistants such as ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini can speed up brainstorming, drafting meta descriptions, summarising crawl exports, writing first drafts of outreach and explaining technical errors. Use them with these rules:

  • Never paste client passwords, personal data or confidential financial information into a public AI tool. Check your client agreement first.
  • Check every fact, statistic, price and legal or medical claim against an official source.
  • Do not publish AI text without editing it for accuracy, tone and originality.
  • Disclose AI use where clients or publishers require it.
  • Keep a prompt library in your Notes app, with the prompts that work for your tasks.

11.6 The free mini-audit

A free mini-audit is a one-page summary of 5 to 8 observations with the most visible issues and simple fixes. It shows your skill and gives the prospect something useful even if they do not buy.

  • Keep it to one page and one hour of work.
  • Use real evidence: screenshots, PageSpeed scores, title tags, Google Business Profile gaps.
  • Include two quick wins they can do themselves and two bigger tasks you can offer.
  • Do not invent problems or exaggerate. Honest audits build trust; false ones end careers.

11.7 Calls and discovery

Ask about goals before you talk about tools. Useful questions include: What is your most profitable service? Where do most enquiries come from now? What have you tried before for SEO? Who handles your website? What would a good result look like in six months? What is your budget range?

11.8 Compliance and ethics

  • Follow anti-spam laws for email, and respect unsubscribe or "no thanks" replies immediately.
  • Do not use other people's private personal data, do not pretend to be a client's staff, and do not impersonate Google or any official body.
  • Do not promise guaranteed rankings or specific income figures.
  • Do not bribe reviewers or ask customers to leave fake reviews.

Pricing, Proposals, Contracts and Delivery

Learning objectives. Choose a pricing model, write a proposal, understand the basic contract terms, and deliver an onboarding that sets up a successful client relationship.

12.1 Pricing models

Prices vary widely by country, experience and the size of the client. As a starting point, many beginners price small local clients at a modest monthly retainer and raise prices as their results and reviews grow. Research what local agencies charge, then price for your time, skills and the value the client receives. Recheck your pricing every six months.

12.2 Simple price calculation

Work out your minimum monthly income, add tax and business costs, divide by the hours you can bill (not all your working hours are billable), and add a margin for growth. Example: you need 1,000 in monthly income, costs are 100, and you can bill 60 hours per month. Minimum rate = (1,000 + 100) / 60 = about 18.30 per hour. Price packages around that minimum plus value and experience.

12.3 Writing a proposal

  1. Their situation: summarise what you learned in the discovery call, in the client's words.
  2. Goals: three measurable goals (for example "increase enquiries from organic search by 20% in six months").
  3. Recommended scope: the tasks, deliverables and timeline for the first 90 days.
  4. What is not included: for example development work, paid advertising, content writing beyond agreed pages.
  5. Price and payment terms: fee, billing frequency, payment method, and what happens if the client pauses.
  6. Reporting: how and when you report, and which tools you use.
  7. Client responsibilities: access, timely approval, and content or product details you need.
  8. Next steps: a single clear action with a date, such as "sign and return by Friday to start on Monday".

12.4 Contracts and key terms

Use a written agreement for every paid engagement, even a small one. A good agreement includes:

  • Parties, service description and deliverables
  • Fees, payment dates, late-payment terms and currency
  • Start date, duration, notice period for cancelling
  • Access and data: who owns accounts, passwords and content; client keeps ownership of their website and data
  • Confidentiality and what you may show in your portfolio (get written permission)
  • Limitation of liability, and a clear statement that SEO results cannot be guaranteed
  • Dispute process and governing law (use the rules of your country)
Get professional adviceThis section is general information, not legal advice. For contract wording, ask a qualified lawyer in your country, or use a reviewed standard template from a professional association.

12.5 Invoicing and getting paid

  • Send invoices on the same day each month, with clear payment terms (for example "due in 14 days").
  • Use invoicing tools such as Wave (free for basic invoicing in some countries), Zoho Invoice or the invoicing tools in your payment platform.
  • Ask for part payment in advance for projects, especially with new clients.
  • Keep records of every invoice and payment for tax purposes. Ask a local accountant about tax registration and deductible business costs.

12.7 Delivering value and keeping clients

  • Respond within one business day, even if the answer is "I will reply by Thursday".
  • Explain what you did and why in simple language. Clients judge value by understanding.
  • Hold a short monthly call to review the report and agree next steps.
  • Admit mistakes early and fix them quickly.
  • Ask for a review or testimonial after a successful milestone, and ask for referrals.
