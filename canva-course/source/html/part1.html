How to Use This Course

This course takes you from opening Canva for the first time to earning money from design work online. It is arranged in fourteen chapters. Read them in order the first time. Later you can jump to any chapter you need.

Each chapter has three kinds of boxes:

Tip. A shortcut or a habit that saves time or improves your work.
Careful. A mistake that beginners often make, or a legal or money point you must not skip.
Exercise. A short task to do before moving on. Doing exercises is the fastest way to learn Canva.

Three rules for learning design

  1. Make something every day, even a small one. Skill comes from making, not from watching.
  2. Keep your files. Name each design with the date and project, for example 2026-11-10 Cafe Menu v2. Your portfolio will grow from these files.
  3. Ask for feedback. Show your work to one or two people who are not your family and ask what is confusing.

Chapter 1

Welcome to the Digital Design Economy

1.1 Why design skills sell today

Almost every business now needs visual content: posts for social media, flyers for events, menus for restaurants, slides for sales meetings, certificates for schools, and product photos for online shops. Most small businesses cannot afford an in-house designer, and many do not know how to use professional software. That gap is your opportunity.

You do not need to be a trained artist to earn from design. Clients pay for clear, attractive, on-time results. A tool such as Canva lets a motivated beginner produce work that looks professional, because the tool supplies good fonts, layouts and colours. Your value is in choosing well, arranging well and understanding what the client needs.

1.2 What a digital freelancer does

A digital freelancer sells a service online and delivers it remotely. Common services for a Canva-based designer include:

ServiceWhat the client getsTypical buyer
Social media graphicsA monthly set of posts, stories and reels coversShops, clinics, coaches, restaurants
Print designFlyers, posters, brochures, menus, business cardsLocal businesses, events, schools
PresentationsPitch decks, reports, training slidesStartups, consultants, teachers
Document designResumes, proposals, certificates, invoicesJob seekers, companies, NGOs
Template creationEditable designs that clients can change themselvesAgencies, online sellers, educators
Simple videoShort animated posts and promo clipsCreators, small brands

1.4 Realistic expectations

Income from freelancing usually starts small and grows over several months. Your first few jobs may be cheap because you are building reviews and confidence. Plan for a gap between starting and earning. Keep a regular job or other income while you learn, if you can. Treat the first year as an apprenticeship: every project teaches you about clients, deadlines and money.

Careful. Be wary of anyone who promises a fixed monthly income after a short course, or asks you to pay them to "unlock" clients. Legitimate training does not guarantee earnings.

Chapter 2

Getting to Know Canva

2.1 What Canva is

Canva is an online design tool that runs in a web browser and also as a phone app. You drag and drop text, images, shapes and templates onto a page, and then download or share the result. It is built for people who are not professional designers, which makes it an ideal starting point for this course.

Canva is used for far more than posters. People make presentations, resumes, websites, videos, documents, whiteboards for meetings, social media calendars and printed products. Many professional designers also use Canva for quick client work and for creating templates.

2.2 Who uses Canva

  • Small business owners who post on social media every week
  • Teachers and students making worksheets and presentations
  • Marketing teams that need many versions of one design
  • Freelancers and agencies who deliver client work quickly
  • Non-profit groups and community organisations

2.3 Canva plans

Canva offers several plans. The names and prices change, so check canva.com/pricing before you decide. In general:

PlanWho it suitsWhat it generally adds
FreeLearning, personal projects, first practiceLarge library of templates, basic photos and elements, core editing tools
Pro (paid)Freelancers and anyone doing paid workPremium templates, photos and fonts, Brand Kit, Background Remover, resizing with Magic Switch, Bulk Create and more AI features, larger storage
Teams (paid)Small teams sharing one brandShared brand folders, team roles, collaboration controls
Enterprise (paid)Large organisationsAdvanced security, administration and support
Education and Non-profitEligible schools and charitiesPro-level features free or at a discount, if you qualify
Tip. Start on the Free plan. Upgrade to Pro only when a paid client needs premium content or the Brand Kit, and when you can see the time it saves you. Canva often offers a free trial of Pro; note the date you start it and cancel before it ends if you are not ready to pay.
Careful: licences. Many Canva photos, templates and fonts are licensed for use in your own designs, with limits. Free and Pro content differ in what you may use commercially. Read the Canva Content Licence on canva.com before you sell a design that contains Canva elements. Chapter 13 explains this further.

2.4 Canva compared with professional software

Canva is quick and easy. Professional tools such as Adobe Photoshop and Illustrator offer far more control, especially for photo retouching and logo vector work. The table below helps you decide when each is the right choice.

TaskCanva is good forUse a professional tool when
Social posts, flyers, menusMost of the timeThe client wants very complex photo edits
PresentationsAlmost alwaysData charts need precise engineering
LogosSimple wordmarks and draftsTrademark-quality, scalable vector logos
Photo retouchingBasic fixes, background removalPortrait retouching or colour grading
VideoShort social clipsLong-form editing or film-quality work

Chapter 3

Creating Your Canva Account

This chapter walks you through creating a secure, professional account. You will need about 20 minutes and access to the email address you want to use for work.

3.1 Before you start

  • An email address for work. Use a plain name such as firstname.lastname@ your email provider. Do not use a joke address; clients will see it.
  • A password manager (the one built into your browser is fine) to create and store a long, unique password.
  • Your phone, in case you need to confirm your identity by text message or an authenticator app.
  • A decision on account type. Canva asks what you will use it for. Choose the option that matches your goal, such as personal or business use. You can change later.

3.2 Step-by-step sign-up

  1. Open a browser and go to canva.com. Click Sign up (or "Get started").
  2. Choose how to sign up. You can use Google, Apple, another single sign-on option, or your email address. If you choose email, Canva will send a confirmation code or link.
  3. If you use email, enter your name, email and a password. Use at least 12 characters with a mix of words, numbers and symbols, or a generated password from your password manager.
  4. Confirm your email. Open the message from Canva and click the verification link or enter the code.
  5. Answer the short questions (for example, your role and what you plan to design). These help Canva suggest templates. Answer honestly; you can skip some questions.
  6. Canva may offer a Pro trial and ask for payment details. You do not need to enter a card to use the Free plan. Choose "Continue with free" or the equivalent if you are still learning.
  7. You land on the home screen. Take a moment to note where the menu, search and account icon are (see Chapter 2).
Careful. Only type card details into canva.com itself, never into a link or message that someone sent you. A message that says "your account will be closed unless you pay now" is almost always a scam.

3.3 Set up your profile

Click your account icon in the top-right corner and open Settings (or "Account settings"). Check these items:

SettingWhat to enterWhy it matters
NameYour real first and last name, or your professional nameClients recognise you; collaborators can find you
Profile photoA clear, friendly head-and-shoulders photoBuilds trust when you share links with clients
Language and regionEnglish, and your countrySets currency, spelling and some template suggestions
Email preferencesKeep product updates on, marketing off if you wishReduces clutter while keeping important notices

3.4 Turn on two-step verification

Protecting your account matters because it may hold client designs and, later, payment information. In the settings, look for Security and turn on two-step verification (sometimes called two-factor authentication). Choose the method you prefer, such as an authenticator app or a text message. Save the backup codes Canva gives you in your password manager.

Tip. Also turn on two-step verification on the email account you used to sign up. If someone gets into your email, they can reset your Canva password.

3.5 Account types you may meet

  • Personal account: one user, one set of designs. Enough for learning and first freelance jobs.
  • Team account: several people share brand assets and folders. You will need this if you hire an assistant or work in a small agency.
  • Education or non-profit: available if you are a teacher, student or registered charity. Requirements change, so check the official page before you apply.

3.6 Common sign-up problems

ProblemWhat to do
The confirmation email does not arriveCheck spam and promotions folders. Wait five minutes, then request a new email.
"Account already exists"You may have signed up with Google or Apple before. Use that button instead of email, or use "Forgot password".
Verification code expiredRequest a new code. Codes expire after a short time.
Page keeps asking you to upgradeClose the pop-up and continue on the free plan. Ignore pressure tactics.
Cannot sign in on a new deviceUse your password manager, or your two-step verification method and backup codes.
Exercise 3. Create your account, enable two-step verification, and write your account email and the date you created it in your notebook (not in a shared file). Then sign out and sign back in to confirm it works.
Chapter 4

Walking Through the Canva Editor

Designing in Canva happens in the editor: the screen where you change a design. This chapter explains each area and the everyday actions you will repeat thousands of times.

4.1 Starting a design

There are three common ways to begin:

  • Use a preset size from the list (for example Instagram Post, A4 Document, Presentation). Canva sets the correct pixel size.
  • Use a custom size when a client gives you measurements. Enter width and height in pixels, or in millimetres or inches for print.
  • Start from a template. Pick a design, then replace the text and images. This is the fastest way to learn layout.

4.2 The editor layout

The five areas you will use most are:

  1. Top bar: file name, undo and redo, resize, download and share.
  2. Context toolbar: changes depending on what you have selected. Select a text box and you see font and size controls; select a photo and you see crop and filter controls.
  3. Left panel: where you find templates, elements (shapes, lines, icons), text styles, your brand assets and your uploaded files.
  4. Canvas: the page itself. Click an item to select it, drag to move, use corner handles to resize.
  5. Page bar: the thumbnails at the bottom. Use this for multi-page documents and presentations.

4.3 Working with text

Click Text in the left panel and choose a heading, subheading or body style, or click the plus-sign to add a text box. Double-click text on the canvas to edit it. Use the context toolbar to change font, size, colour, line spacing and alignment.

Tip. Create your own text styles once (headline, subheading, body) and reuse them. Consistent text styles make your work look professional across a whole project.

4.4 Images, shapes and elements

  • Elements include shapes, lines, frames, icons and illustrations. Search by keyword, for example "coffee icon".
  • Photos are stock images. Check the licence label and whether the photo is marked for Pro users.
  • Uploads hold your own files. Upload logos, product photos and client images here, not from random websites.
  • Frames let you place a photo inside a shape. Drag a photo onto a frame, or use the crop tool.

4.5 Layers, arrangement and grouping

Items stack on top of each other. To move something behind another item, select it and use the Position menu (often in the context toolbar) to bring it forward or send it back. To move several items together, select them with a drag box or with Shift-click, then Group them. Use Lock on a background so it cannot be moved by accident.

4.6 Pages, duplicating and undo

  • Add a page with the plus-sign in the page bar, or duplicate a page with the copy icon.
  • Duplicate an item with Ctrl+D on Windows or Cmd+D on Mac.
  • Undo with Ctrl+Z or Cmd+Z. Redo with Ctrl+Shift+Z or Cmd+Shift+Z.
  • Rename the design by clicking the file name at the top. Use dated names.

4.7 Saving, downloading and sharing

Canva saves automatically as you work. Check the file name and the "saved" indicator if you are unsure. To get a finished file, click Share and then Download. Choose the file type that matches the use:

File typeBest for
PNGSocial posts, graphics with transparency, sharp text on screen
JPGPhotos and smaller web images where file size matters
PDF StandardSharing documents for screen reading, emailing a proposal
PDF PrintPrinting at a shop; ask the printer whether they need bleed or crop marks
MP4 / GIFAnimated designs and short videos
PPTXWhen a client wants to edit a presentation in PowerPoint
Careful. Before you send a design to a printer, check the size, the colours and the resolution with the printer. Screens show colour as RGB; printers usually use CMYK, so bright screen colours can print duller.

Chapter 5

Design Fundamentals You Can Apply in Canva

Canva makes it easy to place things on a page. Good design is about why things are placed where they are. This chapter teaches the principles behind professional-looking work. Each principle can be applied with Canva's tools right away.

5.1 Layout and the grid

A grid is an invisible structure that keeps items lined up. The simplest guide is the rule of thirds: divide the page into three columns and three rows. Place important things, such as a headline or a focal point, on the lines or where they cross. Canva shows helpful guides when you drag items near the edges and centre of the page.

5.2 Four principles every layout follows

PrincipleMeaningPractical step in Canva
AlignmentEverything lines up with something elseUse the guides; align left edges of text blocks; use Align from the toolbar
ProximityRelated items sit close; unrelated items are apartPut a title and its subtitle close together; leave wide gaps between sections
RepetitionUse the same colours, fonts and shapes throughoutSave your colours in Brand Kit or a palette; reuse text styles
ContrastMake important things clearly differentUse bold headlines and light body text, or a dark background with light text

5.3 Typography: fonts and hierarchy

Typography is the art of arranging text. Most beginner mistakes come from using too many fonts, too many sizes, or text that is too small. Use this rule: one or two font families per design, with clear size steps between levels.

5.4 Font pairing and readability

  • Pair a bold, friendly display font for headlines with a simple, clean font for body text.
  • Avoid decorative script fonts for long sentences.
  • Do not mix more than three fonts. Two is usually enough.
  • Give lines room: line spacing of about 1.3 to 1.5 times the font size keeps body text comfortable.
  • Use Canva's font filter to show only fonts that are free for commercial use if you plan to sell designs. Check the licence of each font.

5.5 Colour: palettes and contrast

Colour sets mood and draws attention. A simple way to choose a palette is the colour wheel. Three classic schemes are shown below.

Contrast decides whether text can be read. For body text, aim for a contrast ratio of at least 4.5 to 1 between text and background (this is the common accessibility guideline known as WCAG AA). Dark grey on white or white on deep blue usually passes. Light grey on white usually fails. Canva's colour pickers sometimes show the contrast; if not, use a free online contrast checker.

5.6 Imagery and white space

  • Use strong images. One clear, relevant photo beats three weak ones.
  • Keep the subject visible. Do not place text over a person's face.
  • Leave white space (negative space). Empty areas let the eye rest and make the important items stand out. Beginners usually fill too much space; remove one element and see whether the design improves.

5.7 Accessibility checklist

Accessible design helps everyone, including people with low vision, colour blindness or older screens. Before you deliver any design, check:

  • Body text is at least about 14 pt in print or about 24 px on a phone-sized social post.
  • Contrast is strong enough for text (see 5.5).
  • Colour is not the only way to show meaning; add labels or icons too.
  • Important information is not written only inside a tiny image.
  • Social posts that contain text also include a caption that repeats the key information, for screen-reader users.
Exercise 5. Find a poorly designed flyer or post (your own is fine). Write down three things wrong with it, using the four principles in 5.2. Then rebuild it in Canva with a clear headline, one palette of three colours and at most two fonts. Compare the two versions side by side and note which one you would hire.
