Chapter 6

Hands-On Projects

Theory becomes skill only when you make things. Each project below follows the same pattern: brief, specification, steps, quality check. Work through them in order. Save every finished file in a folder called Practice; these will feed your portfolio in Chapter 8.

Tip. Before you start any project, write a one-line brief: "Who is this for, what should they do after seeing it, and what must be included?" A clear brief makes every decision easier.

Project 1: Instagram square post (1080 × 1080 px)

Brief: A bakery announces a new seasonal product.

  1. Click Create a design and choose Instagram Post.
  2. Set a background colour from your palette. Add a large headline such as "New: Mango Cake". Keep it under six words.
  3. Add one product photo or icon. Place it on a rule-of-thirds intersection.
  4. Add a short line of supporting text and a call to action, for example "Order today".
  5. Check alignment with the guides; check the text is readable on a phone.
  6. Download as PNG.

Quality check: Can you read the headline in one second? Is there one clear focus? Are colours and fonts consistent with your other practice pieces?

Project 2: Social media set (three sizes)

Clients often need the same message in several formats. Canva's Magic Switch (Chapter 7) can do this for you, but first learn the sizes manually.

Steps: Build the square design first. Duplicate it, change the size to Portrait, and adjust the layout. Then duplicate again for Story. Keep text at least about 250 px away from the top and bottom edges of the story so the app's own buttons do not cover it.

Careful. Platforms change their recommended sizes. Check the current guidance on each platform before you deliver to a client.

Project 3: A5 flyer for an event

Brief: A community library runs a free reading day on a Saturday.

  1. Create a Custom size of A5 (148 × 210 mm) or choose A5 from the presets.
  2. Place the event title at the top third. Use the largest text on the page.
  3. Add three essential details only: date and time, place, and what visitors get.
  4. Add a QR code or a website as the call to action. (Canva Apps include QR code tools; check licensing if you use a third-party generator.)
  5. Use a large photo or simple illustration in the lower half, and leave a clear margin around all text.
  6. Download as PDF Print. Ask a printer whether they want bleed (extra colour past the trim edge), usually 3 mm. Chapter 4 explains print files.

Project 4: Professional resume (CV)

Brief: A job seeker wants a one- or two-page resume for an office role.

  1. Search templates for "resume" and choose a clean, single-column design. Avoid heavy graphics and multiple columns, because some applicant tracking systems read them badly.
  2. Replace the sample text with real content, in this order: name and contact, short profile (two to three lines), experience (newest first), education, skills, and languages.
  3. Use action verbs and numbers: "Reduced invoice errors by 30 percent" is clearer than "Responsible for invoices".
  4. Keep the font size readable (about 10 to 11 pt for body) and margins of about 15 mm.
  5. Download as PDF Standard. Save the file with your name, for example Ayesha-Khan-Resume-2026.pdf.
Tip. Make a plain-text version as well, in case an online form cannot accept your design. Copy the content into a simple .txt file.

Project 5: Five-slide presentation

Brief: A small cafe owner presents a plan to open a second branch.

  1. Plan the slides on paper first: title, problem, solution, numbers, call to action.
  2. Choose a presentation template, then remove unnecessary elements. Use one idea per slide.
  3. Use charts from the Elements panel for simple numbers. Label each axis and state the source.
  4. Keep text to about six lines per slide. Put detailed notes in the presenter notes.
  5. Present in full-screen mode to check the timing, then download as PDF or PPTX depending on what the client needs.

Project 6: Simple wordmark logo

Brief: A new home-baking business needs a name logo for a sticker and an Instagram profile picture.

  1. Type the business name. Choose one bold or distinctive font. Canva's font library includes many logo-friendly options; check each licence.
  2. Choose one colour, or two at most. Test the logo in black on white, and in white on the brand colour.
  3. Make a circular or square version for the profile picture. Keep the name readable at small size.
  4. Export as PNG with a transparent background, and as SVG if your plan allows.
Careful: trademarks. Before you deliver a logo, search the name in the official trademark databases of the country where the client operates, and in an internet search. A name that is already registered can cause legal trouble for your client, and possibly for you.

Project 7: Restaurant menu

Brief: A small restaurant wants a printed A4 menu with five sections.

  1. Set up a grid: two columns, equal spacing, consistent item format (name in bold, description in regular, price aligned to the right).
  2. Group items by section (starters, mains, desserts, drinks). Use a clear heading for each.
  3. Keep prices in a consistent currency format and aligned in one column.
  4. Proof-read every item twice. Spelling mistakes in a menu are costly for the client.
  5. Download as PDF Print for the printer, and PDF Standard for emailing.

Chapter 7

Advanced Canva and Magic Studio

Once you can build a basic design, Canva's advanced features help you work faster and deliver more to clients. Some are available only on paid plans. Names and locations of tools change over time, so use the search box in the editor to find them if the menu differs.

7.1 Brand Kit

The Brand Kit stores a client's logo, colour palette, and fonts in one place. Once set up, every new design can use these assets with a single click. This is the single most valuable feature for client work.

  1. Open Brand from the left menu, or from the Brand Kit section of the settings.
  2. Upload the logo in a clean format (PNG with transparent background, or SVG).
  3. Add the brand colours using their exact codes (HEX values such as #7D2AE8). Ask the client for these codes rather than guessing from a screenshot.
  4. Add the brand fonts. You may need the client's font files, or permission to use fonts Canva already provides.
Tip. Create a separate Brand Kit for each client. Keep your own brand as one kit, and name each kit clearly. Mixing clients' assets is a common and embarrassing mistake.

7.2 Magic Studio tools

Magic Studio is Canva's set of AI-assisted tools. The names and availability change, so treat this list as a guide:

Tool (as named in 2026)What it helps withYour check
Magic DesignCreates design ideas from a text prompt or your photosReplace generic text; make sure the look fits the brand
Magic WriteDrafts or rewrites short copyVerify facts, prices and claims yourself
Magic MediaGenerates images or video from textCheck for odd details, copyright risk and the client's rules before use
Magic EditChanges part of an image by describing the changeLook closely at edges and hands or faces
Background RemoverRemoves the background from a photoCheck hair, edges and small objects
Magic SwitchConverts a design to another format or sizeRe-check text placement and crop
Careful. AI output can be wrong, can copy styles too closely, and may not be fully free of third-party rights. Read Canva's terms for AI features, tell clients when AI was used if their contract or their industry requires it, and never let AI write medical, legal or financial claims without a qualified person checking them.

7.3 Bulk Create

Bulk Create (a Pro feature) produces many versions of one design from a spreadsheet. Example: 50 name badges for an event, or 30 product cards with different names and prices. You prepare a CSV file with one row per item, link each column to a text or image spot in your design, and Canva generates a file for each row.

  1. Design one master page with placeholder text such as "{Name}".
  2. Prepare a spreadsheet with a header row and one row per item. Keep it simple: no merged cells.
  3. Upload the spreadsheet and map each column to a text box.
  4. Preview several rows before generating all of them.

7.4 Animation and short video

Canva lets you animate elements (fade, slide, pop) and create short videos. For social media, keep videos under about 15 seconds for the first attempt, with large text and a clear message in the first two seconds. Use the Animate button in the context toolbar and preview with the play button in the timeline. Add background music only from licensed sources, and check whether the platform you publish to requires a separate music licence.

7.5 Canva Websites and Docs

Canva also offers simple website builders and document editors. For a beginner freelancer these are good for a one-page portfolio or a proposal. A professional web host or a dedicated portfolio platform (Chapter 8) may be better for a long-term site, especially if you need a custom domain and search-engine control.

7.6 Collaboration and feedback

  • Share a design with a link, with edit or view permission. Use view-only links for drafts you want clients to comment on.
  • Comments let a client point to an item and ask for a change. Keep a written record of every requested change.
  • Versions: duplicate a design before major changes, and name the copies "v1", "v2", so you can roll back.

7.7 Building a reusable template system

Good freelancers rarely start from zero. Build a folder of master templates for your most common jobs (social post, flyer, menu, presentation). Each one uses your palette and fonts. When a new client arrives, duplicate a master, load their Brand Kit and change the content. This is how you deliver quickly without sacrificing quality.

Exercise 7. Create a free Brand Kit (or use the Brand features in your trial) for a fictional business of your choice. Give it a logo, three colours and two fonts. Build a social post and a flyer that use only those assets, then duplicate the post as a master template named "MASTER – Social Post".
Chapter 8

Building a Portfolio and Personal Brand

A portfolio is your proof of skill. Clients rarely hire a designer on a list of software skills alone; they want to see examples that look like what they need. A focused, honest portfolio of eight strong pieces beats fifty weak ones.

8.1 What clients look for

  • Relevance: examples that match the work they want (for example, restaurant menus for a restaurant client).
  • Clarity: you explain the problem and your solution in a few sentences.
  • Reliability: a professional profile, quick replies, and clean, error-free work.
  • Proof of results: numbers, feedback or testimonials, where possible.

8.2 Choose and structure your projects

Select 6 to 8 pieces from your Practice folder (Chapter 6) and any real work you have done. Use the case study format for each one:

PartWhat to write (two to four sentences)
The problemWhat the client needed, and why it mattered
Your approachKey decisions: audience, palette, layout, message
The outputThe design(s), shown clearly
The resultFeedback, sales, engagement, or "practice project, client to be confirmed"

Be honest. If a project was a practice exercise, label it as one. Never claim a result you cannot support. Never show a client's confidential material without their permission.

Tip. Keep a PDF version of your portfolio at all times. Many clients will ask for one, and some will not click a link from an unknown sender.

8.5 Personal brand basics

  • Choose a niche when you are ready, such as "menus and posters for cafes" or "resumes for nurses". A niche makes marketing easier. You can start broad and narrow later.
  • Create a simple identity: a name or a clear professional title, a profile photo, one colour palette, and one font pair. Use them on your portfolio, your social profiles and your invoices.
  • Write a bio of 50 to 80 words: who you help, what you make, and how you work. Replace it as your skills grow.
  • Be consistent. Use the same name and photo on every platform so clients recognise you.

8.6 Creating samples when you have no clients

Beginners often say, "I have no work to show." Solve this with spec work for practice. Pick a real local business (a cafe, a clinic, a tutor) and create a redesign or sample campaign for it. Label it clearly as an unsolicited concept, for example "Concept redesign for a local cafe, not commissioned". Do not imply that the business hired you. You may later contact them with the concept; many small businesses respond well to a free sample.

Careful. Do not use another designer's work, or a business's logo, as if it were yours. Do not copy a competitor's layout so closely that it could be confused with theirs. Original concepts are safer and better for your reputation.

Chapter 9

Pricing, Finding Clients and Communicating

Pricing and client communication cause most early stress for freelancers. This chapter gives you a method you can adapt to your country, your skills and the market, rather than fixed numbers that would soon be out of date.

9.1 Pricing models

ModelHow it worksBest forRisk
HourlyYou charge per hour workedUnclear or open-ended tasksClients may question time; you earn less as you get faster
Per project (fixed)One price for an agreed scopeDefined jobs like a flyer or menuScope creep if the brief is vague
Per deliverableA price per item (per post, per slide)Social media packs, templatesClients may ask for many small extras
RetainerA monthly fee for an agreed amount of workClients who need ongoing contentNeeds firm limits on revisions and turnaround

9.2 A simple method to set your rate

Work backwards from the income you need:

  1. Monthly income goal (after tax): for example, 1,000 units of your currency.
  2. Add business costs per month: software, internet, phone, portfolio hosting. Example: 100.
  3. Total needed: 1,100.
  4. Billable hours: not every hour of your week is paid, because sales, admin and learning take time too. In your first year, a realistic target is about 50 billable hours a month, even if you work more hours overall.
  5. Hourly rate needed = 1,100 ÷ 50 = 22 per billable hour.

This is a starting calculation, not a final price. Check what similar freelancers charge in your market. Then price by value: a menu that helps a restaurant sell more is worth more to the owner than the hours it took you to design it.

Tip. Increase your rate for every new client, or every three months. Raise prices for new clients first; existing clients usually get notice of 30 days or a clear change at renewal.

9.3 Package tiers

Offer three packages. Clients tend to choose the middle one when it is clearly the best value. Example for social media:

BasicStandard (most popular)Premium
Posts per month81220
Stories or coversNone48
Revisions1 round2 rounds3 rounds
Turnaround5 working days3 working days2 working days
PriceYour base priceAbout 1.5 times baseAbout 2.5 times base

Change the numbers to suit your workload. The point is that every package has a clear limit, so you are never working for free.

9.4 Finding your first clients

  1. Your network first. Tell friends, family, former colleagues and teachers what you can do, and ask them to name one business that might need it. Warm introductions convert best.
  2. Local businesses. Walk into or message shops, clinics and cafes with a sample. Offer a fixed small service at a starter price for the first job.
  3. Online profiles. Post your portfolio on LinkedIn and Instagram. Share one piece of design advice per week, and show your process.
  4. Platforms. Marketplaces (Chapter 10) bring leads, although they take a fee and are competitive.
  5. Referrals. After a good job, ask for a short testimonial and an introduction.

9.5 A simple outreach message

Hello [Name], I am [Your name], a graphic designer who makes menus and social posts for local food businesses. I noticed your cafe's Instagram has great photos but the posts do not show your daily specials clearly. I have made a free sample post for you, attached. If it helps, I can create a set of five posts for next month at a fixed price. Would you like to see more?

Keep outreach short, specific and polite. Mention one thing you noticed about their business, offer something useful first, and make the next step simple. Do not send the same message to hundreds of people; that is spam and it damages your reputation.

9.6 Discovery questions and the brief

Before you quote, ask:

  • What is the goal of this design? (For example: more bookings, more followers, a clear announcement.)
  • Who is the audience, and where will they see it?
  • What must be included? What must be avoided?
  • Do you have a logo, colours, fonts or brand rules?
  • When do you need it, and who approves it?
  • What is your budget?

9.7 Writing a proposal

A proposal confirms the work in writing. Include: your understanding of the goal; deliverables with sizes and formats; the number of revisions; timeline; price and payment terms; what is not included; and an acceptance line the client signs or replies "yes" to. Keep it to one or two pages.

9.8 Revisions and difficult conversations

Revisions are normal, but they must have limits. A common approach: "This package includes two rounds of revisions. Each round means one consolidated list of changes sent in one message. Additional changes are charged at [rate]." When a client asks for something outside scope, respond politely: "That is a good idea. It is outside the current scope, so I can add it for [price] or in the next package."

Careful. Never begin large work without a deposit. A typical arrangement is 30 to 50 percent upfront for a new client, with the rest on delivery. Clients who refuse any deposit on a first project are a risk.

9.9 Worked example

A cafe asks for "some posts for our Instagram". You reply with questions, then offer a Basic package of 8 posts for a fixed monthly fee, with two rounds of revisions and a 50 percent deposit. The cafe chooses Standard instead, because it includes stories for daily specials. You send a proposal, the client signs, you invoice the deposit, and you begin. At the end of the month you ask for a testimonial and a referral.

Chapter 10

Freelance Marketplaces

Marketplaces are websites where buyers and freelancers meet. They can provide a steady flow of first clients, a payment system and a review history. They also take a fee and are very competitive. This chapter explains how to use them well and safely.

10.1 The main platforms

PlatformHow it worksGood forNotes
FiverrYou list fixed-price "gigs" with packages; buyers order directlyClear, small, repeatable servicesFees apply to sellers; many competitors; build reviews early
UpworkYou submit proposals to posted jobs and may pay connects or credits to bidLonger projects and hourly contractsStrong client verification; fees and bidding costs apply
Freelancer.comProjects and contests; you bid on posted workQuick projects and contestsContests can pay little for lots of work; read terms carefully
PeoplePerHourOffers (fixed services) and hourly jobsUK and international clientsFees and verification steps apply
99designsDesign contests and projectsLogos and branding for experienced designersContest model is competitive; a higher-skill market

Platforms change their fees, rules and features often. Check each one's current seller fee and dispute policy before you commit.

10.2 Set up a strong profile

  • Photo: friendly and clear, with good light and no filters.
  • Headline: say what you do and for whom, for example "Menu and social media designer for cafes and restaurants".
  • Description: three short paragraphs: who you help, how you work, and what the client gets.
  • Skills and tools: list Canva and the other tools you use honestly. Add the ones you have actually used.
  • Portfolio: upload 4 to 6 of your best pieces, each with a short title.
  • Verification: complete identity and payment verification as the platform asks. Use the same name on all platforms.

10.3 Create a gig (Fiverr-style)

  1. Title: include your service and the outcome, for example "I will design a 5-post Instagram pack for your cafe".
  2. Packages: offer Basic, Standard and Premium (see Chapter 9).
  3. Gallery: use clear, relevant images and a short video if possible.
  4. Requirements: ask buyers for what you need before starting: logo, colours, text, photos, deadline.
  5. FAQ: answer the questions you are asked most often, such as file types and revision limits.
  6. Delivery time: set a realistic time. Missing deadlines lowers your rating; it is better to promise three days and deliver in two.

10.4 Write winning proposals (Upwork-style)

When you bid on a job, the first two sentences matter most. Show that you read the brief. Example:

"Your menu needs clear sections and readable prices for a printed A4 page. I have designed three restaurant menus in Canva with this structure (samples attached). I can deliver a print-ready PDF within four working days, with two rounds of revisions."
  • Avoid copy-and-paste proposals that ignore the brief.
  • Ask one or two clarifying questions. It shows care and reduces misunderstandings.
  • Bid only on jobs that match your portfolio. Quality bids beat many weak ones.

10.5 Reviews, ratings and reputation

Early reviews are very valuable. To earn them: under-promise, deliver early when possible, respond to every message within a few hours, and ask for feedback politely when the work is complete. Respond to any negative review calmly and factually. Do not argue in public.

10.6 Protecting yourself from scams

Warning signWhat to do
The buyer asks you to move the conversation to WhatsApp, email or another app before hiringKeep the conversation on the platform; that protects both sides
The buyer offers a much higher fee and asks you to pay something firstRefuse. Paying fees to receive work is a scam.
The buyer wants to send a "test" file that you must open or installDo not open unknown files or install software from strangers
Requests for your passwords, bank PIN, or verification codesNever share them with anyone
Payment sent to you from a fake cheque or reversed transferDo not release work until funds are cleared and visible in your account
Careful. Keep all communication, files and payment records on the platform. If a dispute starts, the platform's record is your evidence.

10.7 A weekly routine on marketplaces

  • Monday: review new jobs and send 3 to 5 targeted proposals or respond to enquiries.
  • Tuesday to Thursday: deliver work, make one new piece for your portfolio or gig gallery.
  • Friday: reply to all messages, request reviews, update your profile with your latest work, and record earnings.
