Chapter 11

Selling Templates and Digital Products

Service work trades your time for money. Templates and digital products can earn money while you sleep, because one design can be sold many times. The trade-off is that you must build products before you see income, and marketplaces are crowded. This chapter explains the main routes and the rules you must follow.

11.1 Types of digital product

  • Editable templates: Instagram packs, resume templates, wedding invitations, planners, menu templates. Buyers open them in Canva (or another tool) and change the text.
  • Printables: wall art, planners, worksheets, certificates, flashcards, saved as PDF for buyers to print.
  • Graphic bundles: sets of icons, social post frames, or brand kits you have made yourself.
  • Mini courses and guides: a PDF or video guide on a specific skill, such as "Canva for beginners in 30 days". You may also use this course if you add substantial original material and credit the source.

11.2 Canva templates and the Canva Creators programme

Canva has run programmes that let independent creators design templates and earn when other people use them. Eligibility, the application process, the payment model and the percentage paid all change over time. Visit Canva's creator pages, read the current terms, and do not rely on any figure from a course or a video (including this one) without checking the official source first.

Careful. Canva's own photos, illustrations and elements may not be sold on their own, and may not be included in a product so that a buyer could extract and reuse them separately. Build your templates from your own designs, or from assets whose licence explicitly allows this kind of use. Read the Canva Content Licence and the licence for every font and image.

11.3 Where to sell

PlatformWhat sells wellNotes

11.4 Print-on-demand

Print-on-demand (POD) services print your designs on products such as t-shirts, mugs, posters and phone cases, and ship them when a customer orders. You do not hold stock. You upload designs, set a price, and receive a margin on each sale. Popular POD marketplaces include Redbubble, Society6 and Teepublic, while Printful and Printify connect to your own store.

  • Check each product's print area and file requirements (size, resolution, transparency).
  • Order a sample of your product before you rely on the quality.
  • Design for a niche: a pet, a profession, a hobby. Generic designs are very hard to sell.
  • Respect trademarks. Do not use sports team names, film characters or brand logos.

11.5 Pricing and keywords

For templates, price by value and by effort. A simple Instagram pack might sell for a few dollars or the local equivalent; a full brand kit may sell for much more. Test prices and watch which listings sell. Use specific keywords in titles and tags, for example "editable Instagram template for bakery" rather than "template". Search the marketplace for the terms buyers actually use.

11.6 Quality and buyer support

  • Make your template easy to use. Label editable areas and include a short instruction sheet (PDF).
  • Test the template on a phone and a computer before listing.
  • Include a preview image that shows the real design, not an exaggerated one.
  • Answer buyer questions within a day. Many sales come from reviews.

Chapter 12

Similar Platforms and Transferable Skills

Canva is one tool in a large ecosystem. The principles you learn here (layout, hierarchy, colour, typography, brand consistency) work in every design tool. Learning a second tool is usually faster than learning the first, because you already understand what you are trying to do. This chapter shows how the tools relate and where each one fits a freelance career.

12.1 Platform comparison

Adobe ExpressQuick social graphics and video, similar to CanvaTemplates, text, brand kits, resizingVery easy

Availability, pricing and free tiers for these tools change frequently. Confirm the current position on each vendor's website before you recommend a tool to a client.

12.2 Transferable skills, not just tools

Clients pay for thinking that works in any tool. These skills move with you:

  • Brief reading: turning a client's words into a clear goal and a set of deliverables.
  • Visual hierarchy and layout: the same rules apply in a poster, a slide and a web page.
  • Colour and type systems: palettes and text styles that repeat across files.
  • File management: versioning, naming, exporting to the right format and size.
  • Communication: explaining choices, handling feedback, and closing projects.

12.3 A learning path for after Canva

  1. Months 1–3: Canva for social, print and presentations. Build a portfolio.
  2. Months 4–6: Add one tool that expands your offer. Common choices: Figma (if you are interested in apps and websites), Illustrator (if you want to design logos and icons), or CapCut (if you want to offer short videos).
  3. Months 7–12: Learn the advanced features of your chosen tool, and offer a service that uses it. Keep Canva for speed and templates.
Tip. Each new tool should earn its place. Learn it through a real or practice project, not only by watching videos. Ask yourself: "Which client need does this unlock?"

Chapter 13

Running Your Freelance Business

Important. This chapter gives general guidance. It is not legal, tax or financial advice. Tax registration, invoicing rules, contract law and payment options depend on your country. Consult a qualified accountant or lawyer in your area before you sign large contracts or register a business.

13.1 Register and get set up

  • Register as self-employed or a sole trader with your local tax authority if the law requires it. Many countries have a simple process for individuals.
  • Choose a business name that you are allowed to use and that is not already taken. Check trademark and company registers.
  • Open a separate bank account for business income and expenses. It makes tax records far easier.
  • Keep your receipts for software, equipment and internet bills. Many of these may be deductible, depending on local rules.

13.2 Getting paid

MethodStrengthsWatch for
Marketplace escrow (Fiverr, Upwork and others)Buyer pays first; less risk of non-paymentFees and withdrawal delays; check the rules
Bank transferDirect, low cost for large amountsConfirm the account details by phone or a known channel
Online payment services (PayPal, Wise, Payoneer, others)Works across borders; quick to set upCurrency conversion fees; availability depends on your country
Card payment links (Stripe and similar)Professional invoices with card paymentsRequires account verification and may not be available everywhere
Careful. A payment is not complete until the money is in your account and cleared. Do not start work on a payment that is "pending" or that you have only seen as a screenshot.

13.3 Invoices

Every paid job needs an invoice. Include: your name or business name and contact details; the client's name and details; an invoice number and date; a description of each service; the amount, currency and taxes (if you charge them); payment terms and due date; and your bank or payment details. Number your invoices in sequence (INV-001, INV-002) so your records stay complete.

13.4 Contracts and proposals

For any job above a small amount, use a simple written agreement. It should cover:

ClauseWhat it says
ScopeExactly what you will deliver, sizes, formats and number of items
RevisionsHow many rounds are included, and the process
TimelineStart date, delivery dates and what happens if the client is late with feedback
PaymentPrice, deposit, due dates and late-payment rules
Client responsibilitiesProviding content, brand assets and timely approval
Ownership and licensingWho owns the design, and how the client may use it (see 13.5)
CancellationHow either side can end the work and what is paid for completed work
ConfidentialityHow you will handle the client's private information

13.5 Copyright, licences and fonts

  • Copyright usually belongs to the creator unless a contract transfers it. Most freelancers grant the client a licence to use the final work. Decide the scope (for example, use in one country for one year) and state it in writing.
  • Canva content: templates, photos and elements have licences with limits. Read Canva's Content Licence and the individual licence where it applies.
  • Fonts: check whether the font allows commercial use, embedding in print and in apps. Some fonts require a separate licence for logos.
  • Stock photos and music: keep proof of the licence. Check whether your client's use (print run, paid ads, merchandise) is included.
  • AI-generated images: check the platform's terms and your client's policies before using them in paid work.

13.7 Time, energy and boundaries

  • Set working hours and tell clients when you reply.
  • Block time for admin and marketing, not only for paid work.
  • Take at least one full day off each week. Burnout ends careers quickly.
  • Say no to work that does not fit your skills, budget or schedule. Refer it to someone else if you can.

13.8 Records you must keep

Keep a simple monthly record: every invoice issued, every payment received (with date and platform), every business expense (with receipt), and any taxes you have set aside. Set aside a percentage of each payment for tax as soon as it arrives; the exact amount depends on local rules and should be confirmed with an accountant.

14.2 Learning resources

  • Canva Design School (free, official): short courses on design basics and using Canva. Search "Canva Design School" in your browser.
