# Digital Career Course: Video Series Scripts (English)

Companion to the course PDFs. One video per chapter, Chapter 0 to Chapter 12, released as a series.
This version replaces the short scripts in `Course_Video_Scripts.md`.

## 1. Rules for every video

- One video per chapter. Each video is **up to 5 minutes**. The built English videos run 2.0 to 2.6 minutes each (about 400 to 490 words of narration).
- Each chapter script is written in two parts (A and B). Each part is one voice-over job of about 2 minutes, so the
  voice tool finishes each job well inside its time limit. The two parts are joined into one video with a short pause.
- Pace: about 130 words per minute, with a short pause after each on-screen step.
- Numbers are written as words in the narration (for example "forty-five", "ninety"), so the speech check matches them.
- Style: illustrated explainer with voice-over. Flat drawings from the course PDFs, one idea per screen, no faces.
- Main version 16:9 (1920x1080). Phone cut 9:16 (1080x1920) with larger on-screen text.
- Each video opens with the chapter title card and ends with the chapter checklist on screen.
- Burn the on-screen text in the same language as the narration.
- Background music: one calm loop under the voice at low volume. Sound effects only for a checklist tick.
- Note in every video description: "Powered by Stability AI" if a homidev sound or music loop is used.

### Chapter 1: The digital work world

#### Part A
[SCREEN] Four payment cards appear: per project, per hour, monthly retainer, per task.
Narration: Digital work means work you do on a phone or a computer for a client who may live in another city, or even in another country. Much of it can be done at your kitchen table, in short blocks of time, around your family duties. Many women start this way, and you can too.
Narration: Digital work is paid in a few common ways. The first is a fixed project price. You agree one price for one clear job, such as a set of five social media posts. The second is an hourly rate, where you are paid for the hours you record. The third is a monthly retainer, where a client pays a set amount each month for ongoing work. The fourth is per task, where you earn a small amount for each small item, such as one row of data entry.
Narration: Beginners usually start with projects and small tasks. These are easier to agree on, and they let you finish something quickly. Monthly retainers come later, once you have trust and reviews.

#### Part B
[SCREEN] Map of four places: marketplaces, direct clients, community groups, remote employers.
Narration: Work is found in four main places. Online freelance marketplaces connect you with clients all over the world. Direct clients are people you find yourself, such as a local shop, a clinic, a school or a restaurant. Community groups on Facebook and WhatsApp often post small digital jobs. And remote employers hire part-time staff for support, data and content work.
Narration: Set your expectations for the first year. Your first jobs will be small, and they may pay less than you hope. This is normal. Their real purpose is to give you experience and reviews. Clients rarely hire someone with no reviews, so your first two or three jobs are about building trust, not about big money.
[SCREEN] Learning loop drawing.
Narration: Here is a simple example. Imagine a woman at her kitchen table who writes a short description for a bakery's new menu. The bakery pays her a fixed amount for that task. That is project pay. Next month, the same bakery asks her to send twenty product descriptions, one at a time, and pays for each one. That is per-task pay. If the bakery then asks her to look after its social media every month for a set fee, that is a retainer. The kind of work is the same, but the way you are paid changes the way you plan your time. Before you accept any job, ask how you will be paid, and how long the work will really take.
Narration: Think of your progress as a learning loop. Each job teaches you something, each review makes your profile stronger, and each stronger profile brings a better job. The loop is slow at first, but it speeds up. Work also comes in waves, so keep looking for clients even when you are busy. And some offers are scams. In chapter ten, you will learn exactly how to spot them.

### Chapter 2: Your starting point: device, time and safety

#### Part A
[SCREEN] Checklist appears: device, internet, time, English, space.
Narration: Before you choose a skill, take an honest look at what you already have. You can start with a basic Android phone, or an iPhone with a camera and internet. A laptop helps a lot, but for some skills it is not essential.
Narration: Check three things. First, your device. Second, your internet, either mobile data or Wi-Fi that works most days. Third, your time. Forty-five minutes a day, five days a week, is enough to begin. If you can give one and a half to three hours a day, you will move faster.
Narration: Also check your English. You need to read and write it at a simple level, for emails and client messages. You will improve with practice. Finally, check your space. You need a quiet corner where you can sit for about thirty minutes without interruption. A small table near a window is ideal. It does not need to be a separate room.

#### Part B
[SCREEN] Setup steps appear one at a time: work Gmail, WhatsApp Business, Canva, Google Drive, a reminder.
Narration: Now set up your free workspace. Step one: create one Gmail address that you use only for work. Use a clear name. Step two: install WhatsApp Business, which is free, and fill in your business name, your services and your working hours. Step three: create a free Canva account and a free Google Drive. Make four folders inside Drive: Learning, Portfolio, Clients and Invoices.
Narration: Step four: set a phone reminder for your daily learning time, and keep a notebook beside you. Step five: tell your family your work hours. A short, clear agreement prevents most interruptions.
[SCREEN] Lock icon. Text: "Never share your OTP, PIN or password."
Narration: Now, digital safety. Your accounts are your business, so protect them from day one. Use a strong password of at least twelve characters, and a different password for each service. Turn on two-step verification for Gmail, WhatsApp and any payment account. This sends a code to your phone when someone tries to log in.
Narration: Many women think they need an expensive laptop before they can start. For most beginner skills, this is not true. A mobile phone can do the research, the learning, the designs in Canva, the short video edits in CapCut and the messages to clients. If you share a family phone, agree on a time when you can use it for work, and keep your work apps in one folder. If your internet is weak, download your lessons when you have a good connection, and watch them later. Your time is also a resource. Write down the hours in your week when you are free, even if they are short. Then choose the skill that fits those hours, not the skill that sounds most impressive.
Narration: Here is the most important rule in this course. Never share your one-time code, which we call an OTP, your PIN or your password with anyone. This is true even if someone says they are from a company, a bank or a platform. Real staff never ask for these. Ask for work through the platform, or through a shared Google Drive link that you trust.

### Chapter 3: Choosing your skill

#### Part A
[SCREEN] Skill cards appear one by one, with the free tool under each.
Narration: The skill you choose matters more than anything else in this course. The right skill is one that you can learn with free resources, that you can practise in short blocks, and that people will pay for. Let us look at skills that suit beginners.
Narration: A social media helper creates and schedules simple posts for small businesses, using Canva and WhatsApp on a phone. Canva graphic design means flyers, posters, Instagram posts and menus. Content writing means product descriptions, blog posts and short captions. Virtual assistance means email, calendars, scheduling and simple research.
Narration: Data entry and typing are often the fastest route to a first job, usually in two to six weeks. Video editing with CapCut on a phone suits people who enjoy short reels and captions. Transcription suits careful listeners who type accurately. Online tutoring suits teachers of school subjects or languages. Bookkeeping basics suit organised women who enjoy numbers.

#### Part B
[SCREEN] Three-step box: list what you enjoy, list your time, pick two.
Narration: Here is a simple way to choose. Start by listing the three things you enjoy most, such as writing, colours, talking to people, or organising. Then list how much time you have each day, and whether you own a laptop. Now pick two skills that match both lists.
Narration: Next, spend one week on a free introductory lesson for each of your two skills. Keep the one you enjoy more, and the one you can repeat each day without dreading it. Then commit to that skill for at least ninety days before you change. Changing your skill every week is the most common reason people stay at zero.
Narration: Let us test your choice with an example. Suppose you enjoy writing, and you have one hour a day on a phone. Content writing may suit you, because you can write on a phone, and the practice fits short sessions. Now suppose you enjoy colours, and you have a laptop for two hours in the evening. Canva design may suit you better. Both choices are good. The key question is which skill you can practise every day for the next ninety days without feeling bored or stuck. Ask a friend who knows you well what she thinks you are good at, and write down her answer. Do not decide in one afternoon. Give yourself the week of trial lessons, and then decide with a clear mind.
Narration: Some paths suit certain women. If you want the fastest start, choose data entry, and then move to virtual assistance. If you are creative, choose Canva design, then social media management. If you love language, choose content writing. If you work mainly on a phone, choose video editing. And two skills can work together. A social media helper who also designs in Canva can offer a complete package to a small shop. Write your choice in your notebook now.

### Chapter 4: Your learning route, step by step

#### Part A
[SCREEN] Twelve-week roadmap drawing, first four weeks highlighted.
Narration: This chapter gives you a twelve-week learning route. It works for any skill. Replace the examples with the lessons of your own chosen skill.
Narration: In weeks one and two, build your foundations. Learn how files and folders work: saving, naming, sharing and sending attachments. Learn basic typing and keyboard shortcuts, such as copy, paste and undo. Learn to write a clear message, with a short greeting, one clear purpose and a polite sign-off. Then do the free basics of your chosen skill.
Narration: In weeks two and three, learn your chosen skill. Follow one main course, not five. Watch one lesson, then practise straight away. For every hour of video, plan at least one hour of practice. Keep a short log of what you did each day. This log becomes part of your story when you talk to clients.

#### Part B
[SCREEN] Weekly plan drawing.
Narration: From week three to week eight, practise and build your portfolio. This is the core of the course. Make at least six sample pieces of work, each for an imaginary or real small business. Chapter five explains exactly how.
Narration: From week eight to week ten, find your first clients. Start outreach once you have three samples and a basic profile. Chapter seven gives you the message templates. From week ten to week twelve, deliver and get paid. Your first job should be small and clearly defined. Chapter nine shows you how to deliver it and send your invoice.
Narration: Measure your progress by output, not by hours. At the end of each week, write down what you made and what you learned. Three finished samples are worth more than fifteen hours of watching videos.
[SCREEN] Checklist: four ticks.
Narration: A common mistake is to try to learn everything at once. You may open ten tabs, watch five courses and feel busy, but finish nothing. Instead, pick one course and finish it. Then pick one small project from that course and finish it too. A finished project teaches you more than many unfinished lessons. Use a simple rhythm. Each day, do one lesson and one practice task. Each week, finish one piece of work and share it with one person for feedback. Ask two short questions: what works, and what should I change? Then make the change. This cycle of learn, practise, share and improve is the engine of the whole route. If you miss a day, do not panic. Return the next day and continue.
Narration: Before you move on, check four things. Have you chosen one main skill and written it down? Do you have a learning routine and a weekly plan in your calendar? Have you completed the foundations lessons? And have you made at least one practice piece and shown it to someone for feedback? If you can tick all four, you are ready for chapter five.

### Chapter 5: Building your portfolio with no budget

#### Part A
[SCREEN] Three small business cards: a bakery, a tuition centre, a clinic.
Narration: A portfolio is your proof of skill. Clients buy trust, and a portfolio is how you show it. You do not need paid work to make one. You need six to eight strong samples that look like real jobs.
Narration: Start by choosing three imaginary or real small businesses. A bakery, a tuition centre and a clinic are common and easy to imagine. For each business, create the kind of work it would really need. That might be a flyer, a set of Instagram posts, a reel cover, or a product list.
Narration: If you know a local shop owner, ask whether you may redesign one of their posts for free, in exchange for a review and a credit. Get written permission before you publish their name or logo. Never use a sample you did not create, and never claim a result that did not happen.

#### Part B
[SCREEN] Four hosting options listed on a phone screen.
Narration: Now, where do you host your portfolio for free? You have four good choices. First, a single Google Drive folder, shared as anyone with the link can view. Second, a free Canva design shared as a link. Third, a free LinkedIn featured section, or a free Behance profile. Fourth, a WhatsApp Business catalogue, which clients can open on their phone.
[SCREEN] Checklist: name, samples, review, contact, hours.
Narration: A good portfolio page has five things. Your name, your skill, and one line on what you offer. Three to six samples, each with a short caption that explains the problem and the result. One review, even a short one from a friend, and say honestly if it is a practice project. A clear way to contact you, such as a WhatsApp button. And your working hours, your turnaround time, and the languages you work in.
Narration: Let us look at one sample from start to finish. Imagine a small tuition centre that teaches mathematics to children. Its owner has no poster for the new term. You make a simple poster with a clear headline, with the class times and a phone number. You use one main picture, from a free library, and you keep the colours to three. Then you write a short caption for your portfolio: the centre had no clear poster for the new term, and this design gives parents the times in five seconds. Notice that the caption explains the problem and the result. Clients want to see that you think about their goals, not only about the design.
Narration: Keep quality high. Place the main subject where the lines cross, not in the exact centre. Use no more than three colours and two fonts in one design. Leave empty space around text. Check spelling twice, and read the text aloud once. Finally, test your portfolio on a phone, because most clients will open it there first.

### Chapter 6: Setting up your online profiles

#### Part A
[SCREEN] Profile mock-up with five numbered parts.
Narration: Your profile is your shop window. Clients decide in about thirty seconds whether to read further, so make those seconds count. Here is a simple formula with five parts.
Narration: First, your headline. Say what you do, for whom, in one line. For example: Canva designer for small food businesses, in Urdu and English. Second, your photo. Use a clear, friendly, recent photo, taken in good light from a window. If you prefer privacy, a simple logo or a tidy graphic is also fine.
Narration: Third, your summary. Write three short paragraphs. The first says what you do. The second says who you help. The third says what the client gets. Fourth, your samples. Link your portfolio, and pin your best three pieces to the top. Fifth, your availability. State your hours, your turnaround time, and the languages you work in.

#### Part B
[SCREEN] Platform table: Upwork, Fiverr, LinkedIn, groups.
Narration: Next, freelance marketplaces. Upwork suits writing, admin, data, design and virtual assistant work. You use connects to bid on jobs, so start with small, clearly written jobs. Fiverr suits packaged services with fixed prices, which suits designers and video editors. LinkedIn suits professional clients. Facebook and WhatsApp groups suit local small jobs. Read the group rules first, and never pay to join a job group.
Narration: Check the current terms of any platform before you join. That includes fees, withdrawal options and country availability. Terms change, and this course cannot guarantee them.
Narration: Let us put the profile formula into practice. Write your headline first, even if you change it later. Then write your summary in three short paragraphs. Read each paragraph out loud. If it sounds like a list of words, rewrite it as a sentence that a real person would say to a friend. Next, choose your three best samples, and give each one a short caption. Finally, set your availability honestly. If you can work two hours a day, say so. If you need two days' notice, say that too. A clear profile reduces messages from people you cannot serve, and it builds trust with those you can.
Narration: Start with one platform, not all of them. Complete your profile to one hundred percent. Then, in your first month, apply to about ten carefully chosen jobs. Quality beats quantity. A short, personal proposal that answers the client's exact question wins more often than a copy and paste message. In the next video, we find clients.

### Chapter 7: Finding clients

#### Part A
[SCREEN] Client-hunting funnel drawing.
Narration: Finding clients is a skill you can learn. It is mostly about consistent, polite outreach. Most beginners stop after a few silent replies. This funnel shows why you should keep going. Many messages lead to a few replies, a few replies lead to a sample, and a sample leads to a client.
Narration: Where should you look? Start with local businesses that have weak social media. Salons, bakeries, clinics, tuition centres, boutiques and restaurants are good places to start. Next, think of friends, relatives and neighbours who run a small business, or who know someone who does. Former colleagues who now work in companies are useful contacts too.
Narration: You can also check marketplace job posts that match your skill and budget. And community and professional groups are good places, where people ask for help every day.

#### Part B
[SCREEN] Three message templates appear as cards.
Narration: Personalise every message. Mention one specific thing you noticed about their business, and keep it under eighty words. Here is a template for a local shop. Hello, I am a Canva designer who helps small food businesses with Instagram posts. I noticed that your bakery posts do not show your new menu. I made a sample post for you, with no obligation. Shall I send it?
Narration: Here is a template for a former contact. Hi, I hope you are well. I have started offering design help to small businesses from home. If you know anyone who needs help with social media, I would be grateful for an introduction. I am happy to help you too, anytime.
Narration: Follow up without being pushy. Wait three working days after your first message. Then send one short follow-up that adds value, such as a tip or a free idea. If there is still no reply after a second follow-up, stop and move on. Politeness protects your reputation.
[SCREEN] Tracking sheet preview: date, name, reply, next step.
Narration: Let us look at why a personal message works better than a copy. A copy message says: I can design anything for you. A personal message says: I saw your post about the new menu, and I think the photo would look better in a square frame with your logo at the top. That second message shows that you looked at the business. It is short, specific and friendly, and it gives the owner an easy reason to reply. Before you send, read the message once, and remove any word you would not say out loud. If you are unsure about a business, do not send a message at all. Your reputation starts with the first message you send.
Narration: Set a weekly target. Send fifteen to twenty personal messages a week. Record each one in a simple sheet, with the date, the name, the reply and the next step. Review the sheet every Friday. Recording is what turns outreach into a system.

### Chapter 8: Pricing, proposals and negotiation

#### Part A
[SCREEN] Four-step price formula.
Narration: Many beginners underprice their work out of fear. Underpricing attracts difficult clients, leaves you exhausted, and makes it hard to raise prices later. So set a price you can defend.
Narration: Here is a simple method for your first jobs. Step one: estimate the hours the job will take, and add one round of revisions. Step two: multiply those hours by a fair hourly rate. To find a fair rate, check what similar freelancers charge on the same marketplace, and start in the lower middle of that range.
Narration: Step three: add a small buffer of ten to twenty percent for unexpected work. Step four: compare your price with the value to the client. A sales post that brings in customers is worth more than a plain design. Your first two or three jobs may be discounted to build reviews. Say so clearly, and raise the price for the next client.

#### Part B
[SCREEN] Proposal checklist: problem, deliverables, timeline, price, reason.
Narration: Every proposal should contain five things. First, the client's problem, in their own words. Second, what you will deliver, in clear, countable items. Third, the timeline, with a delivery date. Fourth, the price, the payment method, and the number of revisions included. Fifth, a reason to choose you, such as one relevant sample.
Narration: Now, handling negotiation. Do not drop your price first. Ask what budget the client has in mind. If the price is too high, reduce the scope rather than the rate. For example, you could say: I can do four posts instead of eight for that budget.
Narration: Let us work through a short example. Suppose a shop asks for eight social media posts for one month. You estimate one hour for each post, including one round of revisions. That is eight hours of work. Multiply the hours by your fair hourly rate, which you chose after checking the market. Add a buffer of fifteen percent, and you have your price. Now write the proposal: the problem, the eight posts, the delivery dates, the price, the payment method and the revisions. If the shop says the price is too high, do not cut your rate. Offer six posts instead of eight. You protect your time, and the client keeps control of the budget. This is how professional freelancers stay in business.
Narration: Say no politely when an offer is below your minimum. A clear no is better than a resentful yes. Put every agreement in writing, even in a short chat message. Keep a list of your own minimum rate per hour, and decide it before you talk to clients, so that you are not pressured in the moment.

### Chapter 9: Delivering work, communication and getting paid

#### Part A
[SCREEN] Five-step delivery routine.
Narration: Delivery decides whether a client returns, leaves a review, and refers you to others. Professional habits are simple, and you can start them today.
Narration: Here is your delivery routine. First, confirm the brief in writing. Write down what you will make, for whom, the deadline, the format and the number of revisions. Second, work in short steps, and share a rough draft early. A draft at half way saves a lot of rework.
Narration: Third, deliver the final files in a clear format: PNG or PDF for designs, Word or Google Docs for text, and MP4 for video. Fourth, send a short message that says what you delivered, where the files are, and what you need from the client next. Fifth, ask for a review and feedback once the work is accepted.
Narration: Communication matters too. Reply within one working day, even if your reply is only: received, I will send the draft on Thursday. Keep important decisions in writing, and use the platform's chat or email for anything involving money.

#### Part B
[SCREEN] Invoice layout drawing, then payment route drawing.
Narration: Now, invoices. An invoice is a simple document that asks for payment. It protects you, and it tells the client exactly what to pay. Use a free Google Docs or Canva template. Number each invoice in order, and keep a copy in your Invoices folder. Include your name, the client's name, the items, the total, the payment details and the due date.
Narration: Next, how money reaches you. Marketplace clients pay the platform first, and the platform releases the money to your balance after the job is marked complete. Direct clients may pay by bank transfer, by mobile wallet, or through a payment service. Share only the payment details that you have chosen and set up yourself.
Narration: Let us follow one job from start to finish. The client agrees on a price and a deadline in writing. You share a rough draft on the third day, and the client asks for one change. You make the change, and deliver the final files on the agreed date. You send a short message with the files and a thank you. The client accepts the work. You send the invoice the same day, and the client pays through the agreed method. You then ask for a review. Each step leaves a record: the written brief, the draft, the message, the invoice and the payment. Keep them in one folder for each client, so that you can find any detail in seconds.
Narration: Track every payment in your sheet. Write the date, the client, the amount, the method and the fee. Good records make tax filing and budgeting much easier. Never accept payment in a way that gives you no record. Keep a copy of every receipt, screenshot and transfer confirmation.

### Chapter 10: Staying safe from scams

#### Part A
[SCREEN] Red-flag table appears, one row at a time.
Narration: Digital work attracts scammers who target people who are new and eager. The good news is that the warning signs are clear. Learn them, and you will avoid most problems.
Narration: If you are asked to pay a fee to get a job, join a team, or unlock a payment, it is a scam. Real employers do not charge you to start work. If you are offered very high pay for very easy work, with a quick start bonus, that is also a scam. If a client sends you a cheque and asks you to return part of it, that is fraud. The original payment will bounce later.
Narration: Other signs include a client who wants to move the chat off the platform straight away. They want to avoid the platform's protection, so be careful. Anyone who asks for your OTP, your PIN, your password, or a code sent to your phone is a scammer. Real staff never ask. Be wary of any request to download an unknown app, or to open a file to verify work.

#### Part B
[SCREEN] Checklist: five questions before any new client.
Narration: Before you accept any new client, check a short list. Have you checked the client's profile and reviews on the platform? Do you have a written brief and a clear price before you start? Has anyone asked you to pay anything to start work? Is the payment method one that you set up yourself? And can you say no without feeling pressure? If you cannot answer yes to the first four, or you feel pressure, say no.
Narration: If something goes wrong, act quickly. Stop communicating, save screenshots, and report the job or the user to the platform. Tell a trusted friend or family member before you take any other action. And if money was taken from you, contact your bank or payment provider immediately, and follow their steps.
Narration: Here is a pattern that many new freelancers meet. A new client offers a large job, and asks you to start immediately. They send a payment, but the amount is higher than agreed, and they ask you to send back the difference. This is a common trick. The payment is fake, and the money you send back is your own. Stop, do not return any money, and report the client. Another pattern is a job that needs you to buy software, gift cards, or a starter kit before you begin. Real clients do not ask you to pay them. When you feel rushed, slow down. Scammers depend on speed. Good clients accept a short delay for checks.
Narration: A good client will respect your process. Anyone who pushes you to skip the checks is showing you something important. Next, we look at balancing home and work, and growing your career.

### Chapter 11: Balancing home and work, and growing your career

#### Part A
[SCREEN] Timer graphic: forty-five minutes of work, ten minutes of rest.
Narration: A digital career works best when it fits your life. Your family, your health and your rest matter more than any single job. This chapter helps you protect your time, and grow your income step by step.
Narration: Start by protecting your time. Set fixed working hours, and tell your family and your clients what they are. Use a timer for short, focused blocks, such as forty-five minutes of work followed by a ten-minute break. Keep one place for work.
