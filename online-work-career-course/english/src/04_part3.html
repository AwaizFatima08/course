Your Professional Profile and Portfolio

How to present yourself so that a stranger understands what you do, trusts you and contacts you.

Learning goals. You will build a consistent professional identity across platforms and create a case-study portfolio item that proves your results.

9.1 One identity, many places

Clients often check two or three places before they hire you: the platform profile, LinkedIn and your portfolio. Your name, photo, headline and main service should match on all of them. A consistent identity makes you look established even when you are new.

Figure 9.1 · Anatomy of a strong freelancer profile

9.2 Choosing your headline

Your headline is the first thing people read. Use this formula:

[Who you are] + [what you do] + [for whom] + [proof or specialisation] Examples: "Copy editor for SaaS and e-commerce brands | 40+ articles edited" "Canva designer for social media and small business | 120 posts delivered" "Virtual assistant for busy founders | Email, calendar and CRM support"

9.3 Writing your LinkedIn profile

  • Headline: Same formula as above; LinkedIn allows about 220 characters.
  • About section: Three short paragraphs: who you help, how you work, what you are looking for. End with a way to contact you.
  • Featured section: Pin your 2–3 best case studies or portfolio links.
  • Experience: Add freelance work with a title such as "Freelance Copywriter (self-employed)" and bullet results.
  • Skills: Add 5–10 skills that match your services and get endorsements from people who know your work.
  • Recommendations: Ask past clients or teachers for one short recommendation each.

9.4 Building a portfolio without paid clients

Beginners often think they need a paying client before they can have a portfolio. You do not. You can create spec projects: realistic work for a real or imagined business, labelled clearly as a practice or concept project.

  1. Choose a real business you like (a local café, a small online shop, a non-profit) or invent a plausible one.
  2. Write a brief: goal, audience, deliverable, deadline. Keep it short and realistic.
  3. Do the work to a professional standard and save your process (sketches, drafts, versions).
  4. Write the case study (section 9.5).
  5. Ask for feedback from a community or peer and improve the piece once.
Honesty ruleLabel spec or practice work as such. Do not claim that a client hired you, or invent testimonials. Clients check, and a false claim can end your account.

9.5 The case-study template

CASE STUDY: [Project title] Client / context: [real or practice brief, one line] The problem: [what was not working, in 2 sentences] My role: [what you did exactly] Process: 1. [step] 2. [step] 3. [step] Tools used: [list] Result: [a number if possible: "conversion up 12%", "delivered in 2 days", "reduced editing time by half" - only if true; otherwise describe the quality] What I learned: [1 sentence] Image(s): [before/after, mock-up, screenshot]

9.6 Portfolio hosting choices

Behance or DribbbleDesigners, illustrators, UXFree
Google Drive folder (public link)Writers, VAs, editors who share PDFsFree

9.7 Keeping your profiles current

  • Update your portfolio after every finished project (with the client's permission).
  • Review your headline and skills every three months.
  • Remove skills and samples that no longer represent the work you want.

9.8 Summary

  • Keep name, photo, headline and service consistent on every platform and on LinkedIn.
  • Use a headline formula: who, what, for whom, and proof.
  • Create spec projects if you have no clients yet; label them honestly and write case studies.

Building Proof: Projects, Tests, Certificates and Reviews

How to turn skill into believable evidence that clients can trust.

Learning goals. You will plan a proof strategy: samples, tests, certificates and your first reviews, in the right order.

10.1 Why proof matters more than claims

Every freelancer says "I am detail-oriented and reliable". Clients ignore those words because everyone uses them. What convinces them is evidence: a sample of similar work, a test result, a certificate from a recognised provider, or a review from someone who paid you.

10.2 The proof ladder

Figure 10.1 · Build proof from the bottom up; each rung makes the next one easier to reach

10.3 Certificates: which ones help?

Certificate typeExamplesValue to clients
Platform skill testUpwork Skill Tests, LinkedIn Skill AssessmentsQuick credibility signal on the profile
Vendor or tool certificateGoogle Analytics, Google Ads, Meta Blueprint, HubSpot, Canva design coursesStrong for marketing and design roles
Online course certificateCoursera, freeCodeCamp, Google Digital GarageGood as proof of learning; show the project you made
Formal qualificationDiplomas or degrees in relevant fieldsHelpful for some professional and employment roles
Rule of thumbA certificate proves that you studied. A sample proves that you can do the work. Show the sample, and list the certificate as support.

10.4 Trial work and first clients

Offering a small discounted or trial job can be a sensible way to win your first review. Set limits so that you do not lose money or time:

  • Offer one clearly defined trial (for example, "one 500-word article for $15 with a review request").
  • Never work for free beyond a short paid test, and never accept unlimited revisions.
  • Tell the client what you will deliver, when, and how many revisions are included.
  • Before you start, confirm that the work will be on the platform's contract system (escrow or milestones).

10.5 Collecting reviews the right way

  1. Deliver on time with a short cover message: what is attached, what to check, and how to request changes.
  2. Two or three days later, send a short note: "I hope the work helps. If you are happy with it, would you mind leaving a review on the platform? Thank you."
  3. Never offer payment, discounts or favours for reviews. Platforms can remove reviews and suspend accounts for this.
  4. Reply to every review, thanking the client and naming the result.

10.6 Summary

  • Proof beats claims. Build practice samples first, then tests and certificates, then paid reviews.
  • Use trial work carefully, with clear limits and platform protection.
  • Ask for reviews honestly; never buy or trade them.

  • Referrals: Ask former classmates, colleagues and online community members whether they know a company hiring.

  • Look beyond the big platforms: LinkedIn, company career pages, referrals and communities.

Chapter 12

Writing Proposals That Win

A proven structure for proposals, cover letters and first messages, with examples and common mistakes.

Learning goals. You will write a 150-word proposal that shows you understood the client, with a clear next step, and you will avoid the mistakes that lose jobs.

12.1 Why most proposals fail

Clients often receive 10 to 50 proposals for one job. Most are generic: "Hello, I am an expert in this field with 5 years experience. I can do this for you." Clients skip them within seconds. A winning proposal proves that you read the job, understood the problem and have a clear plan.

12.2 Proposal anatomy

Figure 12.1 · Five parts of a winning proposal, in order

12.3 A proposal template

Hi [Client name], You mentioned that your product descriptions are [their problem, in their words], and that you need them to [their goal]. I recently [similar result: "rewrote 35 descriptions for a skincare shop; their add-to-cart rate rose from 2% to 2.6% in six weeks"]. Here is what I would do for you: 1. Review your top 10 products and your competitors' descriptions (day 1) 2. Write a first draft of 10 descriptions with keyword research (day 2-3) 3. Send one round of revisions after your feedback (day 4) I can deliver the first 10 within 3 working days for [price]. Could you share the product list and the tone you prefer: friendly, premium or practical? [Your name] [Portfolio link]
Write for the readerUse the client's words from the posting, answer their specific questions, and make the next step easy. Read it once aloud before sending.

12.4 Things to do and not to do

DoDo not
Name the client's problem in the first lineStart with "Hello, I am a hard-working expert"
Refer to one specific detail from the job postPaste the same message to everyone
Attach one relevant sample, with a one-line descriptionAttach a dozen unrelated images
Ask one good question about the scopeAsk questions answered in the post
Keep your price within the client's budget, or explain the differenceQuote far above the budget without a reason
Keep all communication on the platformAsk for emails or WhatsApp in the first message

12.5 Cover messages for direct outreach

When you approach a business directly (Chapter 13), keep the message even shorter: two lines about their business, one line about your result, one question. Example:

Hi Sara, I loved your Instagram post about your handmade candles. I noticed your product pages are missing descriptions that mention scent and burn time, which many buyers search for. I rewrote 20 descriptions for a similar candle shop, and their pages gained more organic visits within a month. Would you like me to send a free sample of two of your products?

12.6 Follow-up without being annoying

  • If there is no reply after 3–4 days, send one short follow-up with a new idea or sample.
  • After that, move on. Two follow-ups are enough.
  • When a client says "budget is too low", offer a smaller scope rather than a discount.

12.7 Summary

  • Structure every proposal: problem, proof, plan, price or question, call to action.
  • Personalise at least the first two lines and show you read the post.
  • Keep it short. Clients judge fit in seconds.

Attracting Clients Beyond the Platforms

Direct outreach, LinkedIn, content, referrals and networking that bring repeat clients to you.

Learning goals. You will set up a simple client-attraction system with weekly activities, and you will know how to turn one-time buyers into repeat clients.

13.1 Why you need clients outside platforms

Platforms are an excellent start, but they take a share of every payment, and they put you in a crowd of competitors. Direct clients pay you more, rarely compete with you on price and often become long-term partners. Build a second channel as soon as you have three good reviews.

13.2 The client attraction funnel

Figure 13.1 · From visibility to repeat clients; most of your effort should go into the first two stages

13.3 LinkedIn as a client channel

  • Post twice a week about something you did: a before-and-after, a lesson learned, a small tip for your audience. Keep posts specific and humble.
  • Connect with the right people: founders of small companies, marketing managers, agency owners, and people who have just launched a product. Send a short personal note, never a sales pitch.
  • Comment thoughtfully on posts by your target clients before you ask for anything.
  • Use the Services page to list what you offer and how to start a project.
Connection note example: "Hi Ahmed, I enjoyed your post about your new skincare line. I help small beauty brands write product pages. Would it be useful to connect?"

13.4 Content that attracts clients

You do not need to be famous. You need to be useful to the people who can hire you. Choose one audience and one topic.

Content typeExample for a copywriterHow often
Tip post"3 words that make product titles easier to find"1–2 a week
Case study"How I rewrote 20 candle descriptions in 3 days"Monthly
Behind the scenesYour workspace, your checklist, your draft processOccasional
Question post"What is the hardest part of writing your About page?"Occasional

13.5 Networking that works

  • Join local business groups, chambers of commerce, or online communities where your clients ask questions.
  • Offer help before asking for work. Answer questions, share a free checklist, make an introduction.
  • Tell people clearly what you do. "I help online shops with product descriptions" is easier to remember than "I am a writer".
  • Follow up with everyone you meet within a week.

13.6 Cold outreach with respect

Cold outreach can work when it is specific and polite. Find 10 businesses that match your ideal client each week. For each one, record the business name, the problem you noticed, and the message you sent. Do not send mass mails or pay for lists of unverified emails; these can break anti-spam laws and damage your reputation.

Respect the rulesOnly contact people in ways that the law of their country allows (for example, opt-out rules for marketing emails). Stop contacting anyone who asks you to stop.

13.7 Turning one client into many

  1. Deliver more than you promised, on time.
  2. After delivery, send a short summary of what you did and one suggested next step.
  3. Offer a monthly package (retainer) for ongoing work, such as "10 posts a month" or "monthly SEO check".
  4. Ask for a referral: "Do you know another business that could use this?"

13.8 Summary

  • Build a second channel: LinkedIn, content, networking and respectful outreach.
  • Post about real work to attract the clients you want.
  • Turn every good project into a retainer or a referral.

Chapter 14

Pricing, Contracts, Payments and Taxes

How to price your work, protect yourself with simple agreements, get paid reliably and handle taxes wherever you live.

Learning goals. You will calculate a minimum hourly rate, choose a pricing model, use a basic contract, and know the general tax duties you must check in your country.

14.1 Calculate your minimum rate

Many beginners price by guessing. Instead, calculate the minimum you need:

Monthly living costs (rent, food, internet, phone, transport) = A Savings and emergency fund target (10%) = B Taxes and platform fees (estimate 20-30%) = C Insurance, software, learning budget = D Monthly target income = (A + B + D) / (1 - tax and fee share) Billable hours per month (about 60% of 160 working hours) = H Minimum hourly rate = Monthly target income / H Example: A = $600, B = $60, D = $60, fees and taxes 25% Target = 720 / 0.75 = $960 per month; H = 96 hours Minimum rate = $960 / 96 = $10 per hour
NoteThe example is only arithmetic. Your real costs, tax rate and billable hours will differ. Use the same method with your own figures, and raise your rate as your skills and reviews grow.

Package or gig tiersWork is standard and easy to compareMust be well defined to avoid disputes
Monthly retainerOngoing work for one clientClient may cancel; need a notice period
Value-basedYour work creates measurable business valueHard to prove without data

Figure 14.1 · A price ladder: raise rates in steps as proof and demand grow

14.4 A simple contract checklist

Even for small jobs, agree these points in writing (a platform contract, an email exchange or a one-page agreement):

  • Scope: what is included and what is not, with the number of revisions.
  • Deliverables, format and delivery date.
  • Price, payment method, payment schedule and late-payment terms.
  • Client responsibilities (feedback, materials, approvals) and their deadlines.
  • Ownership and usage rights: when the client receives full rights and whether you may show the work in your portfolio.
  • Confidentiality, cancellation and kill fee (what you are paid if the client stops).
SHORT PROJECT AGREEMENT (template - adapt and check locally) Client: [name, company] Freelancer: [name] Project: [one-sentence scope] Deliverables: [list with formats and quantities] Included revisions:[number] Price and terms: [amount, currency, milestones, payment method] Timeline: [start date, delivery date, feedback deadline] Usage rights: [transfer on full payment; portfolio use permitted: yes/no] Cancellation: [notice period; payment for work completed] Signed (date): [client] [freelancer]

14.5 Getting paid safely

  • On platforms, always use the platform's payment protection (escrow or milestones) and keep work inside the platform.
  • For direct clients, ask for a deposit (commonly 30–50% for new clients) before you start.
  • Use invoices with your business name, the service, the amount, currency, due date and payment details.
  • Track every payment in a simple spreadsheet: date, client, amount, fee, net amount.
  • Never accept a cheque or transfer that you cannot verify, and never send work before a payment is confirmed on a new client.

14.6 Taxes and record-keeping

Freelance income is usually taxable, and many countries require freelancers to register as self-employed or to file returns. Rules vary widely, so this section gives you the questions to ask rather than the answers.

  1. Do I need to register as self-employed, sole trader or a similar status?
  2. Is income from foreign clients taxable where I live, and do I need to report payments from platforms?
  3. Are platform fees deductible, and which other expenses (software, internet share, equipment) can I claim?
  4. Do I need to set aside a fixed percentage of each payment for tax, and when are the deadlines?
  5. Are there social insurance or pension contributions for self-employed people?
Get local adviceSpeak with a qualified tax adviser or your national tax authority's official guidance before your first year of earnings. Keep receipts and records from day one.

14.7 Currency and fees

  • Compare the exchange rate and withdrawal fee of each payment method before choosing it.
  • Price in the currency your clients expect, and state it clearly on every quote.
  • Remember that the platform fee and the currency conversion fee both reduce what you keep.

14.8 Summary

  • Calculate your minimum hourly rate from real costs before you set any price.
  • Use a written scope and simple contract every time; use platform escrow.
  • Track income and expenses from the start, and check your tax duties with local sources.

Chapter 15

Delivering Great Work and Earning Five-Star Reviews

Project management, communication and quality habits that turn one job into a long-term reputation.

Learning goals. You will use a delivery workflow for every project, handle revisions and difficult clients calmly, and recover well when something goes wrong.

15.1 The delivery workflow

Figure 15.1 · Six stages of every project from kick-off to review

15.2 Kick-off checklist

  • Restate the goal, deliverables and deadline in your own words and ask the client to confirm.
  • Collect all materials (logins, brand files, text, photos) before you start.
  • Agree on the feedback method (one consolidated list, not many messages) and the feedback deadline.
  • Set your own internal deadline two days before the client's, to protect quality.

15.3 Communication habits that earn reviews

  • Reply within the time you promised (for example, within 4 business hours).
  • Send a progress update on a long project every two or three days.
  • When you cannot meet a deadline, tell the client as early as possible, with a new date and a reason.
  • Use short, structured messages: what I did, what I need from you, when.

15.4 Handling revisions

  1. Read every revision request twice and list each change as a separate item.
  2. Confirm which changes are included in your package and which are extra work. Quote extras before you start them.
  3. Make the requested changes, then send the file with a short summary of what changed.
  4. If the client keeps changing the brief, pause and agree on a new scope in writing.

15.5 Difficult clients

SituationCalm response
Client says "I do not like it" with no detail"Thank you for telling me. Could you point to the part that is different from what you pictured, so I can fix it?"
Client asks for free extra work"That is a useful addition. I can include it in a new small package for [price], or we can keep the current scope."
Client is late with feedback or payment"Just checking in on the feedback for the draft. Could you confirm when you expect to send it? Our agreed deadline was [date]."
Client is rudeStay polite, keep records, and if it continues, use the platform's support or mediation process.

15.6 When things go wrong

  • Keep all messages on the platform; they are your evidence in a dispute.
  • Use the platform's dispute or mediation process early and calmly, with a summary of what was agreed.
  • If you made a mistake, own it, fix it quickly and say what you changed to prevent it.
  • After a negative review, reply once, politely and briefly; do not argue in public.

15.7 Quality checklist before every delivery

[ ] Brief and every revision request checked off [ ] Spelling, grammar and formatting checked (read aloud once) [ ] File names, format and size correct for the client [ ] Links and images tested [ ] Summary message written: what is attached, what changed, what to check [ ] Review request scheduled for 2-3 days after delivery [ ] Work saved in my archive with the client name and date

15.8 Summary

  • Use the six-stage workflow on every project, including a written kick-off.
  • Communicate early and in short, structured messages.
  • Handle revisions and difficult clients with written scope and calm, specific replies.
