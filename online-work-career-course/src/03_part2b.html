Chapter 7

Other Platforms You Should Know

Freelancer.com, Toptal, Guru, PeoplePerHour, Contra, Malt, Braintrust, portfolio sites, LinkedIn, job boards and AI-training platforms.

Learning goals. You will know what each major alternative is best for, what it expects from you, and how to decide whether to join it.

7.1 How to read this chapter

Each platform below is described by its focus, typical client, how it generally earns money, and what it asks from you. Fee notes are deliberately general: several public sources disagree on exact percentages, so read the official pricing or help page before you join. Platforms are listed in roughly the order a beginner might consider them.

7.2 Marketplaces similar to Upwork and Fiverr

Freelancer.com

  • Focus: A very broad marketplace: web and app development, design, writing, data entry, translation and small local jobs worldwide.
  • Good for: Beginners who want many small projects and a low-entry bidding model.
  • Money: Bidding uses a limited number of free bids and paid bid packs or memberships. Projects carry a commission on payments, commonly reported around 10% with a minimum fee; confirm on the official fee page.
  • Watch out for: Many contest-style or very low-budget listings. Avoid projects that ask you to work before a milestone is funded.

Guru

  • Focus: Long-running marketplace for business, IT, design and writing, with a "Work Diary" time tracker for hourly work.
  • Good for: Freelancers who prefer hourly, tracked work and want a simpler, less crowded market than the largest platforms.
  • Money: Commission is usually described as a smaller percentage than the largest marketplaces, with a membership option. Verify current rates.

PeoplePerHour

  • Focus: UK-founded marketplace with strong demand in design, marketing, web development and writing. Clients are often European.
  • Good for: Freelancers based in the UK or Europe who can work in British English or a European time zone.
  • Money: Commission reportedly decreases as you bill more with the same client, so long-term clients become cheaper to serve. Check the current tiers.

7.3 Vetted and premium networks

Toptal

  • Focus: A highly selective network for software developers, designers, finance and project experts, often for long-term or senior remote roles.
  • Good for: Experienced professionals (usually several years of paid experience) who can pass multi-stage tests and interviews.
  • Money: Clients pay a premium rate. Freelancers are paid their agreed rate; the exact structure is not public in a single headline figure, so confirm during the application.
  • Reality: Expect a long screening process. Use it as a goal to aim for, and prepare a strong portfolio and references.

Braintrust

  • Focus: A talent network with a marketplace-like model that matches freelancers with companies, often for tech and business roles.
  • Good for: Freelancers with a clear specialist skill who prefer matching over bidding.
  • Money: Platform cut and any membership fees should be checked directly.

7.4 Portfolio-first platforms

Behance (by Adobe)

  • Focus: Showcase site for designers, illustrators, photographers and creative teams. Projects are displayed as case studies.
  • Good for: Visibility with art directors and agencies. Free to join.
  • Tip: Upload 3–5 complete projects with a problem, process and result, not just finished images.

Dribbble

  • Focus: Design community and portfolio, especially UI/UX, branding and illustration. Designers also use it to find hiring clients.
  • Tip: Post shots regularly and link to full case studies on your own site.

GitHub and Kaggle

  • GitHub: Host code projects and a clean README for each. Essential for developers and automation specialists.
  • Kaggle: Data analysis notebooks and competitions. Valuable for data analysts and data scientists.

7.5 Professional networks and job boards

LinkedIn

  • Focus: The main professional network. Used for recruiting, referrals, thought leadership and client outreach.
  • Good for: Every career path. Chapter 13 explains how to use it for clients.
  • Features to use: Profile headline, "Open to work" (visible to recruiters only if you choose), Services page, Featured section, Jobs search and Creator mode for posting.

7.6 Portfolio, design and creator-economy sites

  • Contra: A profile-led platform for independent professionals. It is often described as having no commission for freelancers, with clients paying a platform fee. It rewards strong case studies and a clear service page. Verify current terms.
  • Malt: A European platform, strongest for tech, digital and consulting freelancers working with European companies. Check the rate policy and vetting requirements in your country.
  • 99designs, Upwork Talent Cloud and niche boards: Specialist contests and boards for designers, writers, translators and others. Read the terms of any contest-based model; many pay only the winner.

7.7 AI-training and data-annotation platforms

Platforms such as DataAnnotation and Outlier pay people to rate AI responses, label data or review text. They can be a flexible starter income for people with strong English, careful judgment and a few hours a week. However, pay is often variable, screening can be long, and many clones and fake sites exist. Only apply through the official site that you find yourself, never through a link sent to you by a stranger, and never pay anyone to get access.

Before you join any platformRead its terms of service on freelancer obligations, payment holds, dispute rules and account closure. Then check reviews on independent sites and search the platform's name with the word "scam" to see recent complaints.

7.9 Summary

  • Freelancer.com, Guru and PeoplePerHour are broad marketplaces with different fee and client profiles.
  • Toptal and Braintrust are selective; treat them as goals after you build proof.
  • Portfolio sites, GitHub and LinkedIn support every platform you use; keep them consistent.
  • Verify every fee and term on the official site before you sign up.

Skills Required on Each Platform

A practical matrix showing which skills each platform rewards and how to build them.

Learning goals. You will read the skill matrix, identify the gaps in your own skill set, and plan the next 30 days of learning.

8.1 How to use this matrix

Ratings are a guide for a beginner: H = high importance, M = medium, L = low or optional. "Core" skills are those clients test or ask about immediately. Your job is to match at least the core skills of one or two platforms you plan to use.

8.3 Skill detail by path

Writing and editing

  • Core: Grammar, style guides (AP, Chicago or British), SEO basics, research and fact-checking, product or sales copy.
  • Tools: Google Docs, Grammarly or LanguageTool, Hemingway-style readability checks, basic keyword tools.
  • Build proof by: Writing 3 sample blog posts and 3 product descriptions for real or imaginary brands.

Graphic design and social media design

  • Core: Layout, typography, colour theory, brand basics, file preparation (PNG, PDF, print sizes).
  • Tools: Canva (beginner), Figma (UI), Adobe Illustrator or Photoshop, GIMP (free).
  • Build proof by: A 5-design brand kit for one business and a case study of how you made it.

Virtual assistance and admin

  • Core: Email management, calendar scheduling, spreadsheets (formulas, pivot tables), document formatting, confidentiality.
  • Tools: Google Workspace or Microsoft 365, Calendly, Trello, Zapier basics.
  • Build proof by: A sample weekly schedule and a clean spreadsheet dashboard for a fictional business.

Video and audio editing

  • Core: Cutting, pacing, captions, colour correction basics, audio levelling, export settings for each platform.
  • Tools: CapCut, DaVinci Resolve, Premiere Pro, Audacity.
  • Build proof by: Three 30–60 second short-form videos with before and after notes.

Web development and no-code

  • Core: HTML, CSS, JavaScript basics, responsive layout, WordPress or Shopify setup, Git basics, testing on phones.
  • Tools: VS Code, GitHub, WordPress, Shopify, Webflow, Google Search Console.
  • Build proof by: Two live websites and one clone of a real landing page with a short write-up.

Digital marketing and SEO

  • Core: Keyword research, on-page SEO, Google Analytics 4, Search Console, email marketing, ad basics, reporting.
  • Tools: Google Analytics, Search Console, Meta Business Suite, Mailchimp or Brevo, Canva.
  • Build proof by: A 30-day content plan and a monthly report template for a practice site.

Online teaching and tutoring

  • Core: Lesson planning, clear explanation, assessment, a calm and friendly video presence, time-zone planning.
  • Tools: Google Meet, Zoom, Google Slides, Miro or a whiteboard tool.
  • Build proof by: A recorded sample lesson (with permission) and a lesson plan template.

8.4 Skill-building order

  1. Master one core skill to a level where you can deliver a paid job without help.
  2. Add one supporting skill that makes your main service more valuable (for example, a writer who learns basic SEO).
  3. Learn one platform-specific skill (Upwork Skill Tests, Fiverr gig design, LinkedIn content).
  4. Only then consider a second main service. Breadth too early weakens the portfolio.

8.5 Summary

  • Each platform rewards a different mix of skills; read the matrix before you commit.
  • Every core skill needs evidence. Build a sample for each one before you claim it.
  • Learn in this order: one core skill, one supporting skill, then the platform skills.
