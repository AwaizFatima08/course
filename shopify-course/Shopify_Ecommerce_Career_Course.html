Chapter 1

Welcome to E-commerce and Shopify

Before you touch a single setting, you need a clear picture of what an online store is, how money and goods move through it, and where Shopify fits. This chapter gives you that picture and the vocabulary you will use for the rest of the course.

1.1 What is e-commerce?

E-commerce means buying and selling goods or services over the internet. The most common forms are:

TypeMeaningExample
B2C (business to consumer)A business sells directly to a personA clothing brand selling sneakers from its own website
B2B (business to business)A business sells to another businessA packaging supplier selling boxes to restaurants
C2C (consumer to consumer)People sell to each other, usually through a platformSelling second-hand furniture through a classifieds site
MarketplaceOne platform hosts many sellersAmazon, Daraz, eBay, Etsy

Shopify is built for merchants who want to run their own store: a branded website with its own customers, prices, and data. You can still sell on marketplaces through Shopify, but your store is the centre of the business.

1.2 Why Shopify?

Shopify is a hosted e-commerce platform. You pay a subscription, and Shopify runs the servers, security, updates, hosting, checkout and payment processing for you. This matters for a beginner for four reasons:

  • Speed to launch. A working store with a checkout can be live in a day or two, without writing code.
  • Built-in checkout and payments. Shopify Payments and many payment methods are available in one place.
  • Themes and apps. A large theme store and app store cover most needs, from design to email marketing to shipping.
  • Room to grow. The same platform serves a one-person home business and large international brands.

Shopify is not the only option. WooCommerce (WordPress), BigCommerce, Wix and others are alternatives. Learning Shopify well is valuable because it is widely used by merchants and by agencies, which means demand for skilled people is strong.

1.3 How a Shopify store works

Every Shopify store has two sides. The storefront is what customers see. The admin is where you, the merchant, manage products, orders, customers and settings. Figure 1.1 shows the path an order takes from first visit to a happy customer.

Key takeaways

  • Learn the core vocabulary early; it makes every later chapter easier.
Chapter 2

E-commerce Business Foundations

Most stores fail for business reasons, not technical ones. Products nobody wants, prices that do not cover costs, and no plan for getting customers. This chapter helps you choose a sensible model, test demand, and check the numbers before you spend money.

2.1 Choosing a business model

There is no single best model. Each one trades off startup cost, control over the product, margin and effort.

ModelHow it worksStartup costMarginMain risk
DropshippingA supplier ships directly to the customer; you never hold stockLowLow to mediumSlow shipping, thin margins, copycat competition
Print on demandYou design; a partner prints and ships each itemLowMediumQuality control and high cost per item
Own brand (private label)You source a product from a manufacturer and sell it under your nameMedium to highGoodStock risk, minimum order quantities, brand building
Retail resaleYou buy from wholesalers or brands and resellMediumLow to mediumAuthorisation rules, price pressure
Digital productsTemplates, courses, software, printablesVery lowVery highHard to stand out; piracy
SubscriptionsCustomers pay regularly for a product or boxMediumGoodChurn; needs a subscription app

For a first store, a narrow own-brand or print-on-demand range is often the most practical. Dropshipping is possible, but check delivery times carefully; customers who wait four weeks for an order rarely return.

2.2 Market research: proving there is demand

An idea is not a market. Before you buy stock or build a store, look for evidence that people already search for, talk about, and pay for something like your product.

  1. Search demand. Use Google Trends to compare search interest over twelve months. Look for steady or rising interest, not a single spike.
  2. Competitor check. Search the product on Google, Amazon, Daraz or other marketplaces. Many competitors can mean demand, but you need a difference you can explain.
  3. Ad library. Meta's Ad Library shows active ads for any Page. Ads that have run for months are usually profitable.
  4. Customer language. Read reviews of competitors and questions on Reddit, Facebook groups and YouTube comments. Copy the words customers use; they become your headlines.
  5. Small test. Before committing to stock, test with a landing page, a pre-order button or a small ad budget and count real clicks and sign-ups.

Niche scorecard

Score each idea from 1 (weak) to 5 (strong). Choose ideas with a total of 30 or more, and be honest.

CriterionWhat to ask
DemandAre people searching for and buying this now?
MarginCan you sell at a price that leaves at least 30 percent gross margin after product costs?
Size and weightIs it light and small enough to ship cheaply?
Repeat purchaseWill customers need it again, or buy related items?
DifferentiationCan you add a clear reason to buy from you: design, bundle, story, service?
Your knowledgeDo you understand the customer and the product?

2.3 Unit economics: the numbers behind every sale

Unit economics is the cost of making and delivering one sale. If it does not work for one order, scaling will only lose money faster. Figure 2.1 shows a typical breakdown of a 100-unit sale price.

The core formulas are:

  • Gross margin = (Price minus product cost) divided by Price.
  • Contribution per order = Price minus all variable costs (product, shipping, payment fees, packaging, returns, and the ad cost per order).
  • Break-even ROAS = 1 divided by contribution margin percentage. If your contribution margin before ads is 40 percent, you need a return on ad spend above 2.5 just to break even on ads.
Worked example. A phone case sells for 25. Product cost 6, shipping 3, payment fees about 0.9 (roughly 3.5 percent), packaging 0.5. Before ads you keep 25 minus 10.4 = 14.6 per order, a contribution margin of about 58 percent. Break-even ROAS is 1 divided by 0.58, about 1.7. Anything above that is profitable on ads alone. Now do the same calculation for your own niche before you buy stock.

2.4 Legal and administrative basics

Rules differ by country, state and product, so treat this section as a checklist to take to a local accountant or lawyer, not as legal advice.

  • Business registration. Decide whether to operate as a sole trader, partnership or registered company. Registration affects liability, tax and how banks treat you.
  • Business bank account. Keep business money separate. It makes bookkeeping and tax much easier.
  • Tax registration. Find out when you must register for sales tax, VAT, GST or equivalent, and whether it applies to online sales into other regions.
  • Product compliance. Some products need safety testing, labels or certificates, especially toys, cosmetics, electronics and food.
  • Intellectual property. Do not use brand names, logos or copyrighted images you do not own. Check trademarks before you name your store.
  • Consumer law. Most countries require clear pricing, honest descriptions, a cooling-off or return right in some cases, and secure handling of customer data.

2.5 Your first business plan on one page

Write a one-page plan with these headings: the customer (who, what problem, where they shop); the offer (product, price, why you); the numbers (price, unit cost, target conversion rate, monthly ad budget, break-even orders); the channels (how you will find customers in the first 90 days); the risks (supplier delay, returns, a competitor cutting prices) and how you will handle each; and the budget (how much you can lose without damage).

Key takeaways
  • Choose a model that fits your budget and tolerance for stock risk; own-brand or print-on-demand suit many beginners.
  • Prove demand with search data, competitor research and a small test before you commit money.
  • Calculate unit economics first. A sale that loses money cannot be fixed with volume.
  • Handle legal and tax basics early, with local professional advice.
Chapter 3

Setting Up Your Shopify Store

In this chapter you will create a store, configure the settings that every merchant needs, and reach the point where a test order can go through. Work through the steps in order; several later settings depend on earlier ones.

3.1 Creating your account

Go to shopify.com and start a free trial. You will be asked for an email address, a store name and some basic business details. The store name is shown to customers, so choose something short, easy to spell and available as a domain name. Shopify offers several plans (Starter, Basic, Grow, Advanced and Plus). For a beginner the Basic plan is usually enough. Plan prices, features and transaction fees change from time to time, so always check the current pricing page before you decide.

3.2 Choosing and connecting a domain

Your store gets a free address ending in myshopify.com. For a real brand you want your own domain, such as yourbrand.com. You can buy a domain through Shopify or through another registrar and connect it to your store. Shopify provides and renews the SSL certificate that makes your site secure (the padlock in the browser). Pick a domain that matches your brand name and avoid hyphens and numbers where possible.

3.3 Store details and general settings

Open Settings > Store details and fill in your legal business name, address, contact email and phone number. Customers and payment providers check this information. Then open Settings > General to confirm:

  • Store currency: the currency customers see and that you are paid in. Changing it later can affect old orders, so decide carefully.
  • Time zone and units: used for reports, order times and product weights.
  • Customer accounts: decide whether customers must log in to check out or whether guest checkout is allowed. Guest checkout usually converts better.

3.4 The setup checklist

Shopify shows a setup guide on your home page. Figure 3.1 shows the order that works best for a new store.

3.5 Payments

Go to Settings > Payments. The most common route is Shopify Payments, which lets customers pay by major cards and wallets and pays the money into your bank account. Shopify Payments availability depends on your country. If it is not available, you can use a third-party gateway. Complete identity and bank verification, and enable the payment methods your customers expect in your market. Finish verification before launch, because delays here stop payouts.

3.6 Shipping and delivery zones

Go to Settings > Shipping and delivery. Create a shipping zone for each region you serve, then add rates. Common rate types are a flat fee, a rate based on weight, a rate based on order price, and free shipping above a threshold. A simple start is one domestic zone with two rates: standard shipping at a flat fee and free shipping above a threshold that is a little higher than your average order value. Chapter 9 covers shipping in depth.

3.7 Taxes

Go to Settings > Taxes and duties. Shopify can collect tax automatically in some regions, or you can set your own rates. Enter your tax-inclusive or tax-exclusive pricing preference. Check with your accountant which tax applies to your products, whether prices should include tax, and when you must register. Do not guess here; tax mistakes are costly and hard to unwind.

3.8 Policies and legal pages

Go to Settings > Policies. Shopify can generate a refund policy, privacy policy, terms of service, shipping policy and contact information page. These templates are a starting point. Read them, edit them to match what you actually do (for example, your return window and who pays return postage), and have a professional review them before you launch in a regulated market. Policies are shown at checkout and in your footer, and many payment providers require them to be live.

3.9 Staff accounts

If you work with a partner or assistant, invite them under Settings > Users and permissions. Give each person the lowest access they need. A customer-service assistant does not need access to payouts or billing. Use strong passwords and turn on two-factor authentication for every account with admin rights.

3.10 Testing before launch

Before you remove the password page, place a full test order on the storefront. Use Shopify's test payment method for development stores, or a real low-value order that you refund. Check that: the confirmation email arrives, the order appears in the admin, the tax and shipping amounts are right on mobile and desktop, and the refund works. Then open Online Store > Preferences and remove the password protection when you are ready.

Common mistake. Launching with incomplete policies, untested payments or wrong shipping rates. Customers will find these problems in minutes and leave, and payment providers may pause your account. Finish the checklist first, then launch.
Key takeaways
  • Choose a plan, a short memorable store name and a domain you can use for years.
  • Complete store details, currency, payments, shipping, taxes and policies before launch.
  • Place a full test order and refund it before you remove the password page.
  • Give team members only the permissions they need.
Practice task. Create a development or trial store. Complete steps 1 to 4 of the checklist in Figure 3.1 and write down anything you could not finish and why. Keep the notes; they become your launch checklist in the capstone (Chapter 14).
Chapter 4

Navigating the Shopify Admin

The admin is your control room. Merchants who know it well can find an order in seconds, spot a problem before a customer complains, and make changes without calling support. This chapter walks through the main areas and builds a daily routine.

4.1 The layout

The left sidebar holds the main sections. The top bar has search, notifications and your store switcher. Figure 4.1 is a simplified sketch; the exact labels and order can change as Shopify updates the admin, but the logic stays the same.

4.2 Orders: the most important screen

Orders go through two separate statuses that beginners often confuse:

  • Payment status: Paid, Pending, Refunded or Partially refunded.
  • Fulfilment status: Unfulfilled, Fulfilled or Partially fulfilled.

A paid, unfulfilled order is your work queue. Use the filters to show only those orders, and sort by oldest first. Tags help you group orders, for example gift, wholesale or check address. When you open an order, you can see the customer, items, shipping address, payment details, timeline and notes. Add an internal note whenever you contact a customer about an order, so a colleague can see the history.

4.3 Customers and segments

Each customer record stores orders, addresses, marketing consent and notes. Segments group customers by rules, for example "bought more than twice in 90 days" or "spent over a set amount". Segments are useful for email campaigns and for judging loyalty. Respect consent: only email people who agreed to marketing messages, and honour unsubscribe requests immediately.

4.4 Products, collections and discounts

The Products section is covered in Chapter 5, and the Discounts section in Chapter 8. In brief: discount codes are typed by the customer at checkout; automatic discounts apply when the cart meets conditions; and you can set limits on use, minimum purchase, dates and eligible products. Test every discount in a private browser window before you announce it.

4.5 Settings you will use often

SettingWhat it controls
Store detailsBusiness name, address, contact information shown to customers
PaymentsPayment providers, payment methods, payouts
Shipping and deliveryZones, rates, pickup locations, packing slips
Taxes and dutiesTax collection, rates, duties for international orders
CheckoutCustomer information requirements, marketing opt-in, order tips
NotificationsEmails sent to customers and staff (order confirmation, shipping, refunds)
PoliciesRefund, privacy, terms and shipping policies
MarketsCountries, currencies and languages you sell in (Chapter 12)
Users and permissionsStaff accounts and what each can access

4.6 The Shopify mobile app

The Shopify mobile app lets you check orders, reply to customers, view sales and receive alerts while away from your desk. Use it for monitoring, but do bulk changes such as product imports and theme edits on a computer, where you can see more detail.

4.7 A daily routine

  1. Morning (15 minutes): check sales and sessions on the home page, then open unfulfilled orders and clear anything paid more than 24 hours ago.
  2. Midday (10 minutes): answer customer messages, check for low-stock alerts, and confirm that today's shipments left.
  3. Evening (10 minutes): check failed payments, refund requests and any reviews that need a reply.
  4. Weekly (45 minutes): review the Analytics reports, top products, discount performance and shipping costs (Chapter 11).
Common mistake. Treating "Paid" as "done". A paid order that is not packed and shipped is a customer waiting for a parcel. Your fulfilment queue is more important than your sales dashboard.
Key takeaways
  • Learn the sidebar sections and where each setting lives.
  • Track payment status and fulfilment status separately.
  • Use filters, tags and notes to run orders without losing track.
  • Build a short daily routine; consistency beats occasional long sessions.
Practice task. In a development store, create three test orders, mark one as fulfilled, refund part of one, and add a tag and an internal note to each. Then use the filters to show only unfulfilled orders.
Chapter 5

Products, Collections and Inventory

Your product pages do most of the selling. Clear data, good photos and honest descriptions turn visitors into buyers. This chapter explains how Shopify structures products, how to write and photograph them, and how to keep stock accurate.

5.1 Anatomy of a product

A product in Shopify has a title, description, media (images and video), pricing, inventory, shipping details, organisation fields and search-engine fields. Here are the fields that matter most:

FieldPurpose and advice
TitleClear and specific, for example "Classic Cotton T-shirt, Relaxed Fit". Avoid keyword stuffing.
DescriptionBenefits first, then features, materials, sizing and care. Chapter 5.4 gives a template.
Price and compare-at pricePrice is what customers pay. Compare-at price shows a crossed-out original price. Use it only when the reference price was real.
SKU and barcodeYour internal codes. Keep them unique; they link stock to orders and to suppliers.
Options and variantsSize, colour, material and so on. Each combination becomes a variant with its own price, stock and SKU.
InventoryTrack quantity per location. Turn off tracking only for items you truly never run out of.
WeightNeeded for accurate shipping rates. Enter the packed weight if you can.
Product type, vendor, tagsUsed for filtering, collections, automation and reports.
MetafieldsExtra custom data, such as fabric care codes or size charts, shown on the storefront when a theme supports them.
URL handle and SEO fieldsThe page address and the title and description shown in Google. Chapter 10 covers SEO.

5.2 Options and variants

Use variants when a customer chooses between versions of the same product. Use separate products when the item is genuinely different, such as a T-shirt and a hoodie. Figure 5.1 shows the structure for a T-shirt sold in three sizes and two colours.

Common mistake. Creating a separate product for every colour. It splits reviews, weakens search rankings and makes the catalogue harder to manage. Use colour and size variants instead.

5.3 Collections

Collections group products for navigation and merchandising. There are two types:

  • Manual collections contain products you add by hand. Use them for hand-picked lists such as "Editor's picks".
  • Automated (smart) collections add products that match rules, such as product type equals "Hoodie", or tag equals "new-arrival". Use them for categories that must stay current.

Keep your navigation to five or six top-level collections. Too many choices make shoppers stop and think, and thinking reduces purchases.

5.4 Writing descriptions that sell

Use this structure for every product description:

  1. Headline benefit in one line: what the product does for the customer.
  2. Short paragraph that describes the situation the customer is in and how the product helps.
  3. Bullet points with specifications: material, dimensions, weight, care, origin.
  4. Sizing help: a size chart and a sentence on fit (for example, "Runs slightly large; size down for a fitted look").
  5. Reassurance: delivery time, return policy link, warranty if any.
Example. Weak: "Nice blue water bottle, 750 ml." Better: "Stay hydrated all day without the plastic taste. This 750 ml stainless steel bottle keeps drinks cold for 24 hours and hot for 12. Leak-proof lid, fits most car cup holders. Dishwasher safe."

5.5 Product photography

Photos are the strongest sales tool you have, and they cost less than most people expect. You need:

  • At least one clean studio shot on a plain background, with consistent lighting and the product filling about 80 percent of the frame.
  • Shots of the product in use, on a person or in a real setting.
  • Detail close-ups: texture, stitching, buttons, labels.
  • Scale reference: the product beside a common object, or a size-on-model shot with the model's height stated.
  • A short video if possible. Customers watch video longer and it often lifts conversion.

Use a square or 4:5 ratio, export at a sensible size (around 2000 pixels on the long side, compressed to a few hundred kilobytes), and name files descriptively, such as classic-tee-black-front.jpg. Smaller files make the store faster, which matters for both search rankings and sales.

5.6 Inventory and stock control

Inventory is tracked per location. If you have one warehouse and a home office, add both as locations and assign stock to the place it is stored. Set a low-stock threshold so Shopify warns you before you sell out. Use the CSV import and export tools for bulk changes: export your products, edit in a spreadsheet, and import again. Always back up the export before a large import.

Overselling happens when stock is wrong. Count your physical stock at least once a month, reconcile it with the admin, and record every return that goes back on the shelf.

5.7 Pricing basics

Price from your unit economics (Chapter 2), not from what a competitor charges. Then test: prices ending in 9 or 99 often perform well in many markets, but test with your own customers. Use compare-at prices honestly. If you show "was 3,000, now 2,000", the 3,000 must have been a real recent price. False discounts damage trust and can breach consumer law.

Key takeaways
  • Fill in every product field that customers, search engines or your reports use.
  • Use variants for sizes and colours; use separate products only for different items.
  • Write benefit-led descriptions with specs, sizing and reassurance.
  • Photograph the product clearly, in use and in detail; compress images.
  • Keep stock accurate per location and count physically each month.
Practice task. Create one product with two options and at least three variants. Write a description using the five-part structure, add three photos (or three placeholder images), set a low-stock threshold, and export your product list as a CSV file.
Chapter 6

Store Design with Themes

Design builds trust in seconds. A clean, fast, easy-to-navigate store makes customers feel safe buying from you. This chapter covers choosing a theme, using the theme editor, applying simple design principles, and a short introduction to the code behind a theme, so that you can talk to developers in their language.

6.1 Choosing a theme

Shopify themes are templates that control layout, typography and colour. You can use free or paid themes from the Shopify Theme Store. Dawn is Shopify's free, modern reference theme and a very good starting point. When you compare themes, check:

  • Speed. Test the demo on mobile with Google PageSpeed Insights. Heavy themes with many animations can slow your store.
  • Mobile layout. Most sales now happen on phones. Open the demo on a small screen and check the menu, product page and checkout button.
  • Sections. Modern themes are built from sections you can add, remove and reorder without code. Check that the sections you need exist.
  • Support and updates. Look at the developer's update history and documentation.
  • Fit. The demo should look like the customer you want to reach. A luxury jewellery theme will not suit a budget tool shop.

6.2 The theme editor

Go to Online Store > Themes > Customize. The editor shows a live preview on the left and settings on the right. The important ideas are:

  • Templates are page types: home page, product, collection, cart, search, blog, page and 404. Each template can have different sections.
  • Sections are blocks of content such as a hero banner, product grid, testimonials or FAQ. You add, reorder and configure them.
  • Blocks are items inside a section, such as one slide in a slider or one review card.
  • Header and footer hold your logo, navigation menus, announcement bar and legal links. Menus are created under Navigation.
  • Theme settings control global items: fonts, colours, buttons, spacing and icons.

Always click Duplicate on the live theme before you experiment. A copy lets you edit safely while customers keep seeing the original.

6.3 Design principles that convert

You do not need to be a designer to make good choices. Follow these principles:

  1. Clear hierarchy. The eye should go first to the headline, then to the product or offer, then to the button. Use size, weight and spacing to show this order.
  2. One main action per screen. On a product page the main action is "Add to cart". Make it the most visible button.
  3. Whitespace. Empty space makes premium products look premium and helps busy shoppers read.
  4. Contrast and readability. Dark text on a light background, at least 16 pixels for body text on mobile, and enough contrast for people with low vision.
  5. Consistency. Use two fonts at most, a palette of four or five colours, and the same button style throughout.
  6. Trust signals. Show reviews, delivery times, return policy and secure payment logos close to the buy button.

6.4 Speed and mobile performance

Speed affects both search ranking and conversion. Aim for pages that load their main content within about two to three seconds on a typical mobile connection. The main levers are:

  • Upload images at the size they display, not the size they were taken. Use the theme's image settings and avoid giant hero images.
  • Install fewer apps. Every app can add scripts to every page. Remove any app you do not use.
  • Limit custom fonts to two families and only the weights you use.
  • Use video sparingly, and never autoplay with sound.
  • Test on a real phone over mobile data, not only on your office Wi-Fi.

6.5 Understanding a theme's code

You do not need to write code to run a store, but understanding the basics helps you brief developers, check changes and fix small issues. Shopify themes are built with Liquid, an open template language, mixed with HTML, CSS and JavaScript. Liquid shows data and controls what appears:

<h1>{{ product.title }}</h1>
<p>Price: {{ product.price | money }}</p>

{% if product.available %}
  <button>Add to cart</button>
{% else %}
  <p>Sold out</p>
{% endif %}

{% for image in product.images %}
  <img src="{{ image | image_url: width: 800 }}" alt="{{ image.alt }}">
{% endfor %}

The three kinds of tag are: {{ ... }} outputs a value, {% ... %} runs logic such as if-statements and loops, and | passes a value through a filter such as money.

A theme is a folder of files with a standard layout:

theme/
  assets/      CSS, JavaScript, images, fonts
  config/      settings_schema.json and settings_data.json (theme settings)
  layout/      theme.liquid (the outer HTML wrapper for every page)
  locales/     translation files (en.default.json and others)
  sections/    reusable sections that merchants can add and edit
  snippets/    small reusable pieces of Liquid (icons, buttons)
  templates/   page templates: index, product, collection, cart, page, 404 ...

Developers work on a theme locally with the Shopify CLI, using commands such as shopify theme dev to preview changes on a development store and shopify theme pull and shopify theme push to sync files. Use version control (Git) for any custom theme work so you can roll back a bad change.

Common mistake. Editing the live theme's code directly. A single syntax error can take the whole store down. Duplicate the theme, test the copy on a preview link, then publish.
Key takeaways
  • Choose a theme for speed, mobile layout, sections and fit with your brand. Start from Dawn if unsure.
  • Use templates, sections and blocks in the theme editor to build pages without code.
  • Design for hierarchy, one main action, whitespace, readability and trust.
  • Keep the store fast: few apps, sized images, limited fonts.
  • Know Liquid's basic syntax and the theme folder structure so you can work with developers.
Practice task. Duplicate your theme. Build a home page with five sections: announcement bar, hero banner, featured collection, reviews and footer. Check it on a 375-pixel-wide mobile preview. Write down three changes you would make to improve clarity.
Chapter 7

Apps, Integrations and Customisation

Apps extend your store with features Shopify does not include out of the box: reviews, email tools, subscriptions, accounting connections and more. Used well, they save hours. Used carelessly, they slow the store, clash with each other and leak money every month. This chapter shows you how to choose, manage and automate apps wisely.

7.1 What apps do

Apps are software from Shopify or independent developers that connect to your store through Shopify's APIs. They can add a page, a checkout feature, a background process or an integration with an outside system. You find them in the Shopify App Store, and you install them from the admin with one click. Most apps offer a free plan or a free trial, then charge a monthly fee.

7.2 How to choose an app

Before installing an app, run through this checklist:

  1. Do you need it now? If a manual process works for the next month, wait.
  2. Is there a built-in feature? Shopify's own features (for example discounts, Shopify Email, or basic reports) are often enough.
  3. Reviews. Read recent reviews, not just the average rating. Look for complaints about support, billing or broken features.
  4. Last update and developer. Check when the app was last updated and whether the developer has a clear support route.
  5. Total cost. Add the monthly fee, any per-order charges and the cost of the plan needed to unlock features.
  6. Speed impact. Test your store speed before and after installing. Remove apps that add slow scripts for little benefit.
  7. Permissions. Read what data the app requests. Install only apps that need data relevant to their job.
  8. Exit plan. Find out how to remove the app and whether your data stays with you if you leave.

Chapter 8

Payments, Checkout, Taxes and Policies

Checkout is where money changes hands, and most lost sales happen here. This chapter explains how payments work, how to make checkout smooth and trustworthy, how to think about tax and fraud, and why your policies are part of your sales process.

8.1 How payments work

When a customer pays, the money goes from the customer's bank or wallet, through a payment provider, to your bank account. The provider charges a fee and holds funds for a short period before paying out. Shopify offers Shopify Payments, which is built into the store, or you can connect third-party gateways. Each gateway has its own fees, supported countries and payout schedule. Compare them before choosing, and check the total cost including the fee on each transaction and any extra fee for not using Shopify Payments.

  • Payment methods include cards, digital wallets, Shop Pay (Shopify's accelerated checkout), and local methods depending on the country. Offering the methods your customers already use increases conversion.
  • Payouts are the transfers from the provider to your bank. Know the schedule so you can plan stock purchases.
  • Chargebacks and disputes happen when a customer asks their bank to reverse a payment. Keep clear delivery evidence, respond to disputes on time and keep your order records tidy.

8.2 The checkout journey

Figure 8.1 shows the standard path. Every extra step, field or surprise cost is a point where people leave.

8.3 Making checkout convert

  • Allow guest checkout so people do not need an account to buy.
  • Show total cost early. Surprise shipping or tax at the last step is one of the top reasons carts are abandoned. Show delivery cost on the product or cart page.
  • Ask only for what you need. Each extra field costs conversions. Phone numbers are needed for some carriers; ask for them only if you use them.
  • Offer express payment. Accelerated checkout buttons shorten the journey for returning customers.
  • Use trust signals near the payment step: secure payment icons, clear return policy link, customer support contact.
  • Customise checkout carefully. Shopify's checkout can be branded and extended through its checkout extensibility features. Keep changes minimal and test on mobile.

8.4 Abandoned carts

Many shoppers add items and leave. Shopify records abandoned checkouts and can send recovery emails. A typical recovery sequence has three emails: a reminder within a few hours, a message that addresses a likely objection (delivery, returns, sizing), and a final message with a clear reason to return, such as a limited offer you genuinely honour. Do not discount on the first reminder; you train customers to wait for discounts.

8.5 Taxes

Tax rules are complicated and vary by country, state and product. In outline, you need to know:

  • Whether your prices include tax or tax is added at checkout.
  • Which tax rate applies to each product and each destination.
  • When you must register and begin collecting tax.
  • Who pays import duties on international orders (you, or the customer at delivery). This is set in the Markets and duties settings (Chapter 12).

Shopify's tax settings can calculate and collect tax automatically in supported regions, and apps can help where Shopify's built-in options are not enough. Still, the responsibility for correct filing stays with you. Keep records of every order, and review tax settings with an accountant at least once a year and whenever you launch in a new country.

8.6 Policies that protect you and the customer

Clear policies reduce disputes and make customers feel safe. Each should be short, specific and easy to find.

PolicyWhat it must say
Refund and returnHow long customers have to return items, the condition required, who pays return shipping, how refunds are paid, and any exceptions (for example, personalised items)
ShippingWhere you ship, typical delivery times, cost basis, what happens if an order is delayed or lost
PrivacyWhat personal data you collect, why, who receives it (for example, carriers and payment providers), and how customers can access or delete it
Terms of serviceRules for using the store, pricing and error corrections, governing law
Contact informationBusiness name, address, email and phone, and how fast you reply

Your return policy must match what you actually do. A policy that promises a 30-day return and then refuses after 14 days is worse than having no policy at all.

8.7 Fraud and risk

Fraud appears as stolen cards, false refund claims and fake addresses. Shopify provides automated fraud analysis that flags risky orders. Review flagged orders manually, especially high-value orders, first orders from unfamiliar locations and orders where the shipping address differs sharply from the billing address. Never ship a flagged order without checking. Also be cautious with very large first orders, unusual quantities and rush shipping requests from new accounts.

Common mistake. Hiding shipping or tax costs until the final step. Customers who feel tricked leave, and some write negative reviews. Show the true total early, even if it is less attractive than a low product price.
Key takeaways
  • Compare payment providers on total cost, payouts and supported methods.
  • Make checkout short, transparent and fast on mobile; allow guest checkout.
  • Use abandoned-cart emails that help customers rather than rushing them into discounts.
  • Check tax rules with a professional; keep order records.
  • Write clear, honest policies and review flagged orders before shipping.
Practice task. Go through your own checkout on a phone as if you were a customer. Count the steps and fields, note where the total cost first appears, and list three changes that would reduce friction without removing anything you legally need.
Chapter 9

Shipping, Fulfilment and Returns

Customers judge your store by the parcel that arrives at their door. Fast, accurate, well-packed delivery builds repeat business; late or damaged orders create refunds and bad reviews. This chapter covers shipping rates, fulfilment options, the daily dispatch process and handling returns.

9.1 Shipping rates

Shipping costs can destroy margin if they are set without thought. Choose a model that fits your product:

  • Flat rate: one fee for everything. Simple, good for light, uniform products.
  • Weight-based: the fee rises with the parcel's weight. Good when weights vary widely.
  • Price-based: the fee depends on the order total. Useful for free-shipping thresholds.
  • Carrier-calculated: live rates from carriers, shown at checkout. Accurate but needs setup and can confuse customers.
  • Free shipping above a threshold: a strong conversion tool. Set the threshold slightly above your average order value so customers add one more item.
  • Local pickup: lets customers collect from you or a partner location, which can cut costs.
Example. Average order value is 1,800. Set free shipping at 2,000. Customers who are 200 short often add a sock pair or a second item, raising average order value while you still cover your shipping costs.

9.2 Fulfilment models

ModelHow it worksBest forWatch out for
Self-fulfilmentYou store, pack and ship from home or a small unitStart-ups with low volumeYour time becomes the bottleneck
Third-party logistics (3PL)A warehouse stores and ships for you, integrated with your storeGrowing stores with steady ordersMinimum fees, contract length, returns handling

9.3 The fulfilment process

Figure 9.1 shows a reliable dispatch routine. Whatever your model, every order should pass through the same steps.

9.4 Packing and the unboxing experience

Good packing protects the product, reduces returns and creates a memorable first experience. Use boxes sized to the product, not oversized boxes filled with air, which cost more to ship. Include a packing slip, a thank-you note, care instructions and a clear returns route. Check every order against the slip before sealing the box. Photograph high-value or fragile orders as evidence in case of transit damage.

9.5 Tracking and communication

Turn on shipping confirmation and delivery update emails in Settings > Notifications. Include the tracking link and a realistic delivery window. When a parcel is delayed, contact the customer before they ask. Proactive updates prevent most "where is my order" tickets.

9.6 Returns and refunds

Returns are a cost of doing business in e-commerce. Plan for them rather than being surprised. A good returns process has five parts:

  1. A clear policy (Chapter 8.6) that customers can read before buying.
  2. A simple request route, such as a form or email with the order number.
  3. An approval step to check the reason and condition of the item.
  4. A returns label or instructions, with a tracked route for high-value items.
  5. A refund or exchange issued promptly, plus restocking if the item is resaleable.

Track return reasons. If "wrong size" is the top reason, improve size guides. If "not as described" is high, improve photos and descriptions. Returns data is one of the best free sources of product improvement ideas.

Common mistake. Refunding customers without tracking the reason. You then keep paying the cost of the same problem without learning anything. Log every return reason, even if you only look at the list monthly.
Key takeaways
  • Choose a shipping rate model that protects your margin; use free-shipping thresholds to lift order value.
  • Pick a fulfilment model that matches your volume and time, and grow into 3PL when the work demands it.
  • Run a fixed dispatch routine every day, and send tracking automatically.
  • Design a simple, fair returns process and learn from return reasons.
Practice task. Build a shipping table for a product that weighs 300 grams. Include a flat rate, a free-shipping threshold and a note on who pays returns. Calculate the margin on one order after shipping, packaging and returns cost.
Chapter 10

Marketing Your Store

A beautiful store with no visitors earns nothing. Marketing is how strangers learn about your brand, trust it enough to buy, and return. This chapter explains the marketing funnel, the channels that matter for a Shopify store, how to start paid ads safely, and how to plan a year of promotions.

10.1 The marketing funnel

Customers move through stages. Each stage needs different messages and different measurements. Figure 10.1 shows the four stages and typical channels.

Beginners often pour all their budget into the top of the funnel and ignore loyalty. Most profit in e-commerce comes from repeat customers, because acquiring a new customer is far more expensive than selling again to an existing one.

10.2 Search engine optimisation (SEO)

SEO brings free, ongoing traffic from Google. For a Shopify store, the basics are:

  • Product and collection titles that describe what the customer searches for, such as "Stainless Steel Water Bottle 750 ml".
  • Meta titles and descriptions written for people, not robots: clear, specific, under about 60 and 155 characters respectively.
  • Unique product descriptions, not copied supplier text. Duplicate content is a common problem for stores that sell many similar items.
  • Image alt text describing each picture, which helps accessibility and image search.
  • Fast pages and mobile-friendly design (Chapter 6).
  • A blog or guide section that answers real questions, for example "How to choose the right water bottle size". Publish consistently and link to relevant products.
  • Redirects when you delete or rename a page, so old links do not lead to error pages.

SEO takes months. Measure progress in Google Search Console (free) and track organic sessions in your analytics.

10.3 Social media and content

Short-form video is the most powerful organic channel for many product categories. Platforms such as Instagram, TikTok, YouTube Shorts and Pinterest reward useful, entertaining or surprising content. Good formats for product stores include:

  • Demonstrations: the product solving a problem in 15 to 30 seconds.
  • Before and after results, shown honestly.
  • Behind the scenes: packing orders, sourcing trips, design process.
  • User-generated content: real customers using the product, with permission.
  • Answers to common questions: sizing, care, durability.

Hook the viewer in the first three seconds with a question, a result or a surprising fact. Post consistently, reply to comments, and use the platform's own features rather than just reposting elsewhere.

10.4 Email and SMS

Email is the most profitable channel for many stores because you own the list. Build these automated flows first:

  1. Welcome series (three to four emails): your story, best sellers, reviews, and a first-order incentive if you can afford it.
  2. Abandoned cart (Chapter 8.4).
  3. Post-purchase: order confirmation, shipping update, care tips, review request, then a cross-sell after the product is likely to be used up.
  4. Win-back: for customers who have not bought in 90 to 120 days.

Use Shopify Email or an email app to build these flows. SMS can be powerful for time-sensitive messages, but you must get clear consent and follow local rules about marketing texts.

10.5 Paid advertising

Paid ads give fast results but cost money every day. The main platforms are Meta (Facebook and Instagram), Google (Search and Shopping), TikTok, Pinterest and YouTube. Start small and learn:

  1. Set a test budget you can afford to lose completely. Decide the maximum you will spend before you judge results.
  2. Make several creatives: different hooks, formats and product angles. Creative quality is the biggest driver of results on social platforms.
  3. Use the structure: campaign (objective), ad set (audience and budget), ad (creative and copy). Keep one clear goal per campaign.
  4. Track properly: install the platform pixel or connect the official Shopify channel app, and check that purchases are recorded correctly.
  5. Judge by return on ad spend (ROAS) against your break-even figure from Chapter 2, not by clicks alone.
  6. Pause losers and scale winners slowly. Increasing budget by large jumps often resets learning and hurts results.
Worked example. You spend 1,000 on ads and receive 3,500 in sales from them. ROAS is 3.5. If your contribution margin before ads is 40 percent, your gross profit from those sales is 1,400, which is more than the 1,000 spent, so the campaign made a profit of 400 before overheads. Your break-even ROAS is 1 divided by 0.40, which is 2.5.

10.6 Influencers, affiliates and partnerships

Creators can introduce your product to an audience that already trusts them. Choose creators whose audience matches your customer, check their engagement rather than follower count, and agree on clear deliverables, usage rights and a tracked discount code or link. Affiliate programmes pay a commission on sales and carry less upfront risk, but they attract opportunists unless you set terms carefully.

10.7 Planning promotions across the year

Build a yearly calendar of moments that matter to your customers: seasonal holidays, religious festivals, back-to-school, national days, sales events, and your own anniversary. Plan content and stock six to eight weeks ahead of each. Be guided by your target market, because the calendar differs by country. Avoid constant discounting; a sale that happens every month stops being a sale and starts being your price.

Common mistake. Scaling ad spend before the product page converts. If visitors do not buy, more traffic only means more wasted money. Fix the store (Chapters 5, 6 and 8) before you pour in budget.
Key takeaways
  • Think in a funnel: awareness, consideration, purchase and loyalty, each with its own channels.
  • SEO and short-form video build long-term traffic; email builds ownership of your audience.
  • Start paid ads with a capped test budget, several creatives, and ROAS against your break-even.
  • Plan promotions across the year and avoid permanent discounting.

Chapter 11

Analytics, Conversion and Growth

Good merchants run the store by numbers. They know which channels make money, where visitors give up, and which change is worth making next. This chapter teaches the core metrics, shows how to read Shopify's reports, and gives a method for improving conversion step by step.

11.1 The reports you will use

Shopify Analytics is built in. The home page shows a summary, and the Analytics section holds detailed reports. Start with these:

  • Sales over time: revenue, orders and average order value by day or week.
  • Acquisition: where sessions come from (direct, search, social, email, paid).
  • Behaviour: which pages visitors view and how far they go in the funnel.
  • Product performance: units sold, revenue and returns per product.
  • Customers: new versus returning customers, and repeat purchase rate.
  • Marketing: spend, sales and return on ad spend by campaign, if you connect your channels.

For deeper analysis, connect Google Analytics 4 and the Meta pixel or Shopify's official channel apps, so every platform records purchases the same way. Expect small differences between platforms; they count attribution differently. Pick one source of truth for money (the Shopify orders report) and use the others for trends.

11.2 Metrics that matter

MetricFormulaWhat it tells you
Conversion rate (CVR)Orders divided by sessionsHow well the store turns visits into sales
Average order value (AOV)Revenue divided by ordersHow much each order is worth
Revenue per visitorRevenue divided by sessionsCombines CVR and AOV in one number
Customer acquisition cost (CAC)Marketing spend divided by new customersWhat it costs to win a buyer
Return on ad spend (ROAS)Ad-attributed revenue divided by ad spendEfficiency of paid campaigns
Customer lifetime value (LTV)Average profit per customer over their relationship with youHow much you can afford to spend to acquire

Rule of thumb. A healthy LTV to CAC ratio for a mature e-commerce brand is often quoted as about 3 to 1, but this varies by margin, category and repeat rate. Use it as a question to ask, not as a target to hit blindly.

11.3 Reading the funnel

A conversion funnel shows where visitors leave. Figure 11.1 uses sample numbers from a small store in one month.

Read the funnel from top to bottom and ask one question at each step. A large drop from product view to add to cart suggests a product page problem: weak images, unclear price, missing reviews or poor delivery information. A large drop from checkout started to order suggests checkout friction or unexpected costs. A low sessions figure with a healthy funnel is a traffic problem, which points to marketing (Chapter 10).

11.4 Conversion improvements, in order

  1. Speed. Fix slow pages on mobile first. This benefits every step.
  2. Product page clarity. A clear title, price, delivery estimate, size guide and sharp photos.
  3. Social proof. Reviews with photos, ratings near the buy button, and short testimonials.
  4. Trust. Visible returns policy, secure payment, real contact details and a physical business address.
  5. Offers. Free-shipping thresholds, bundles and first-order incentives, tested for margin.
  6. Checkout. Guest checkout, express payment options and no surprise costs.

Test one change at a time. Use an A/B testing app or a controlled test, run it until you have enough orders to trust the difference, and keep notes of each result. Changes that seem obvious often fail, and small changes sometimes win big.

11.5 Building a weekly review

Every week, spend 45 minutes on this routine:

  1. Compare sessions, orders, revenue, AOV and CVR with last week and the same week last year.
  2. Check the funnel for the biggest drop and write one hypothesis.
  3. Review the top ten and bottom ten products by revenue and return rate.
  4. Check ad performance against break-even ROAS, and pause anything clearly losing money.
  5. Read ten customer messages or reviews and note recurring themes.
  6. Choose one action for the coming week and assign a date to it.
Common mistake. Changing five things at once and then not knowing which one worked. Also avoid judging a test after a day or two. Small stores need time and volume before results mean anything.
Key takeaways
  • Know the core formulas: CVR, AOV, CAC, ROAS, LTV, repeat rate and abandonment.
  • Use the funnel to find the biggest drop, then form and test one hypothesis at a time.
  • Use the orders report as the source of truth for money.
  • Run a short, consistent weekly review.
Practice task. Using the numbers in Figure 11.1, calculate the abandonment rate, and if AOV is 2,000, the monthly revenue. Then write down the one change you would test first and how you would know it worked.
Chapter 12

Scaling, International Sales and Retail

Once a store has steady orders, growth comes from new markets, new channels and better operations. This chapter introduces Shopify Markets for international selling, B2B wholesale, sales channels beyond the website, and the habits that let a small team handle more orders without losing quality.

12.1 Selling in more than one country

Shopify Markets lets one store sell in several countries. You set up a market for each region and control its currency, language, prices, payment methods, shipping and duties. Figure 12.1 shows the structure with example markets.

Before you expand, check these points:

  • Demand and competition in the new country. Use the same search and ad-library research as Chapter 2.
  • Shipping cost and speed. International parcels can cost more than your product margin allows. Test with a limited range first.
  • Duties and import rules. Some products face high duties, restrictions or certification requirements. Decide who pays duties, and state it clearly.
  • Returns from abroad. Return costs can exceed the value of the item. Many stores set no-return rules for low-value international orders, but this must be clear before purchase and allowed by local law.
  • Language quality. Machine translation without review damages trust. Use a professional or native speaker for product copy and policies.
  • Payments and local preferences. Offer the payment methods people use in that country.

12.2 Wholesale and B2B

Business customers buy differently: larger quantities, negotiated prices, invoices and payment terms. Shopify's B2B features (available on higher-tier plans) support company accounts, customer-specific catalogues, price lists and wholesale ordering. Even on a smaller plan, you can handle wholesale enquiries by email with a simple price list and a minimum order quantity. Set minimums that cover your handling costs, and check that wholesale pricing does not undercut your own retail prices in a way that damages the brand.

12.3 Sales channels beyond your website

Shopify connects your catalogue to several places where customers already shop:

ChannelGood forTrade-off
Shopify POSPop-up shops, retail stores, market stalls, eventsNeeds hardware and inventory discipline across locations
Social shopping (Instagram, Facebook, TikTok, Pinterest)Impulse buys from contentPlatform rules and changing features
Google shopping listingsShoppers already searching for your productRequires clean product data and competitive pricing
Marketplaces (Amazon, eBay, Daraz and others, via apps or connectors)Reach and trust among shoppers who start on marketplacesFees, competition, strict rules, less brand control
Wholesale and B2BVolume ordersPricing discipline and credit risk

Add channels one at a time. Each one needs product data, stock sync and customer service. A new channel that sells out your stock and leaves you unable to fulfil orders damages your reputation everywhere.

12.4 Operations that scale

  • Write standard operating procedures (SOPs) for every repeated task: adding a product, handling a refund, responding to a delivery complaint. A checklist lets someone else do the job correctly.
  • Use a shared inbox for customer messages with clear response targets, such as first reply within one working day.
  • Automate the repeatable with Shopify Flow and apps, but keep a human check for refunds, disputes and high-value orders.
  • Track stock weekly and set reorder points based on lead time and sales speed.
  • Protect cash flow. Stock purchases often happen weeks before revenue arrives. Forecast cash needs monthly and never commit more to stock than you can pay for from the next weeks of sales.
  • Hire or outsource slowly. Start with a virtual assistant for order admin or content, then add specialists when a task fills a full working week.
Key takeaways
  • Expand internationally only after checking demand, shipping, duties, returns and language quality.
  • Shopify Markets manages currencies, languages and prices for each market from one store.
  • Wholesale and new sales channels add reach but add operational load; add them one at a time.
  • Scaling depends on SOPs, clear service standards and careful cash-flow planning.
Practice task. Choose one country that is not your home market. Research demand for one product using search data and competitor listings, list three shipping or duty issues you would have to solve, and write a one-page go or no-go recommendation.

Always write a scope of work that lists deliverables, exclusions, timelines, the number of revision rounds, payment terms and who owns the content and accounts at the end.

13.8 Interview and client conversation basics

Interviewers want to know whether you can solve their problems. Prepare short, concrete answers to these questions:

  • "How would you improve our product page?" Describe the funnel, one hypothesis, the change, and how you would measure it.
  • "Walk me through setting up a new store." Use the checklist in Chapter 3, and mention policies, payments and testing.
  • "A customer says their order is late. What do you do?" Check the tracking, contact the customer proactively, offer a clear next step, and log the issue to find a pattern.
  • "Which metrics do you look at first?" Sessions, conversion rate, AOV, margin, and ROAS, in that order, with a reason for each.

13.9 A realistic first-year plan

  1. Months 1 to 3: Complete this course, build three demo stores, join the Partner Program and document each project publicly or in a private portfolio.
  2. Months 4 to 6: Complete the redesign case study, do one pilot project for a real business, and apply for 20 relevant roles or pitch 20 local businesses.
  3. Months 7 to 9: Take one paid project or one junior role. Specialise in one area you enjoy most and keep learning in that area.
  4. Months 10 to 12: Gather testimonials, raise your prices for new clients, and build a repeatable process: intake form, scope, build checklist, launch checklist and handover.

Results depend heavily on consistency, communication and the quality of your work. Be cautious of any claim that promises fast or guaranteed income from e-commerce. The businesses that succeed usually make careful choices, track their numbers and keep improving.

Key takeaways
  • E-commerce careers range from store operations and marketing to development and agency ownership.
  • Build a portfolio of three demo stores, one case study and one real project.
  • Specialise, price for value, and write clear scopes of work.
  • Prepare concrete answers in interviews and client conversations, backed by the metrics from Chapter 11.

Chapter 14

Capstone Project and 90-Day Action Plan

The final chapter brings everything together. You will launch a small but complete store, measure it, present it as a portfolio piece, and follow a 90-day plan that turns this course into a working habit.

14.1 The capstone brief

Build a store for one product category of your choice. You may use a development store or a live store with a small test budget, as long as you respect local law and do not sell prohibited items. Your deliverables are:

  1. Business plan (one page), following Chapter 2, with unit economics for at least three products.
  2. Store setup completed through all nine steps in Chapter 3.
  3. Catalogue of 10 to 15 products, with variants, complete descriptions, photos and SEO fields (Chapter 5).
  4. Theme customised with a home page, product template, collection page and mobile check (Chapter 6).
  5. Apps: no more than three, each justified in writing (Chapter 7).
  6. Policies, shipping and tax settings reviewed against Chapters 8 and 9.
  7. Marketing plan: one welcome email sequence of three emails and one short video script (Chapter 10).
  8. Analytics setup and a weekly review template, with one hypothesis tested (Chapter 11).
  9. Presentation: a five-minute walk-through (video or slides) explaining the store, the numbers and what you would do next.

14.4 Self-check quiz

Answer these questions without looking back. The answers follow.

  1. What is the difference between payment status and fulfilment status in the admin?
  2. Name the three parts of a Liquid tag and what each does.
  3. Write the formula for conversion rate and for break-even ROAS.
  4. Why should you duplicate a theme before editing it?
  5. Name two reasons to avoid installing many apps.
  6. When should you offer free shipping, and what threshold logic is useful?
  7. What is the main difference between a manual and an automated collection?
  8. Why might a store with a healthy funnel still have low sales?
Answers.
  1. Payment status shows whether money was received (paid, pending, refunded). Fulfilment status shows whether the items were shipped (unfulfilled, fulfilled).
  2. {{ ... }} outputs a value; {% ... %} runs logic such as if-statements and loops; | passes a value through a filter such as money.
  3. CVR = orders divided by sessions. Break-even ROAS = 1 divided by contribution margin percentage (before ads).
  4. So customers keep seeing the working live version while you test, and you can roll back if the edit breaks something.
  5. Apps add scripts that slow the store, cost money every month, can conflict with each other, and create more logins and risk.
  6. Offer free shipping when it raises order value enough to cover the shipping cost. Set the threshold slightly above the average order value so customers add a small extra item.
  7. A manual collection holds only the products you add by hand; an automated collection adds products that match rules and updates itself.
  8. The conversion rate can look healthy while total sales stay small, because the store gets too few visitors. Check sessions and audience as well as ratios. If visitors arrive but do not buy, look at price, offer clarity and product-market fit.
